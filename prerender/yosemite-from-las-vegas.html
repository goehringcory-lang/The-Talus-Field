A great many first trips to Yosemite begin at an airport in Nevada. Las Vegas is where the cheap flights land, where the Southwest road trip starts, and where a visitor who has already seen the Grand Canyon and Zion looks at the map and notices Yosemite. The map is not wrong. What it hides is that there are two entirely different drives between the two places, that the calendar chooses which one you get, and that the shorter, more beautiful one does not exist for about half the year. Nobody planning from a routing app in February finds that out until the road sign at Lee Vining tells them.

So here is the trip in the order the decisions come: the number to plan around, the pass, the summer road and the winter road, where to sleep on the way, and the version of the trip that works.

The number that decides everything

The Park Service's own directions page gives two figures for Las Vegas, and the difference between them is this article. In season, by US 95 and Highway 120 over Tioga Pass, it is 400 miles and about eight hours to Yosemite Valley, and the page dates that road June through October. In winter, by I-15 and Highway 41 through Bakersfield and Fresno, it is 495 miles and eight to ten hours. Both figures are to the Valley floor in clear conditions, with no stops that matter. Both are longer than the routing app will admit, because the app does not know that the last stretch of either road, seventy-odd miles from Lee Vining or ninety-odd from Fresno, is slow mountain highway with a fee station and a line at the end of it.

Treat eight hours as the floor, not the estimate. Yosemite Valley is about seven miles long, and a time quoted to the Valley means its west end; Curry Village, the trailheads, and the east-end lots are another fifteen or twenty minutes on, plus parking. The honest planning number, either season, is a full day behind the wheel, and it does not leave room for a day trip. Yosemite from Los Angeles is a two-night trip at six hours each way. From Las Vegas it is a two-night trip at the very least, and the version worth the fuel is three.

Eight hours in season, ten in winter, and the road itself changes with the month. Whatever else you plan, plan the pass first.

The question to ask before any other: is Tioga Pass open?

Everything about this trip hangs on one road. Highway 120 crosses the Sierra at Tioga Pass, 9,945 feet, the highest highway pass in California, and it is the only way into Yosemite from the east. The Park Service's standing description is that Tioga Road is typically open to vehicles from late May or June until sometime in November. In 2026 it opened on May 15, unusually early. The closing end has no date at all: it is a judgment made on a forecast when the first serious snow arrives, and in the last five seasons it fell between October 21 and November 15. An itinerary that crosses the pass in the last week of October is a bet. One that crosses it in December is not an itinerary.

While the pass is open, Las Vegas to Yosemite is a desert-and-mountain drive that arrives in the high country and drops into the Valley from above. While it is closed, the trip is a drive around the southern end of the Sierra Nevada that arrives through the South Entrance, and it shares its last two hundred miles with the Los Angeles route. They are not variations on one drive, and the rest of this piece takes them one at a time.

Check the road before you book anything, and again the night before you leave. The park's road line is 209/372-0200, then 1, then 1; the park's current conditions page and the Park Bulletin carry the same status, and the conditions page collects the webcams and forecasts around it. The Park Service's own warning about navigation applies here with more force than anywhere else in California: the park has no single address, phone apps have routed winter visitors toward the closed pass for years, and if a road sign and a screen disagree, the sign is correct.

The summer road: two ways to reach US 395

The season road has a fixed final act, the crossing from Lee Vining over the pass, and two ways to reach it from Las Vegas. They are very different drives, and the difference is Death Valley.

The Park Service's road, through Tonopah. This is the 400-mile version the directions page quotes. US 95 runs north out of Las Vegas through the empty middle of Nevada to Tonopah; from Tonopah, US 6 heads west to Benton, just inside California, and Highway 120 continues west to US 395 a few miles south of Lee Vining. It is a fast, nearly featureless road through sagebrush basin and range country, and it has the great virtue of being the shortest. It also has almost nothing on it. Towns are far apart and the services in them are small, so the fuel discipline is simple: fill the tank whenever a pump appears and you are below half, and carry water and food.

Through Death Valley. The other road leaves US 95 at Beatty and enters Death Valley National Park on Nevada 374, or leaves Las Vegas by Pahrump and enters on California 190 from Death Valley Junction. Either way it crosses the park on Highway 190, past Stovepipe Wells and Panamint Springs, and comes out at Olancha on US 395, from where the road north runs through Lone Pine, Bishop, and past Mammoth Lakes to Lee Vining. It is somewhat longer than the Tonopah road and slower per mile, and in the right month it is one of the great drives in the country: a second national park on the way to the first, at the lowest and hottest end of the state on the way to one of the highest.

The right month is the problem. Death Valley's summer is not a warm spell; it is the hottest weather on the continent. The park reports average July highs of 116°F at Furnace Creek, temperatures that often top 120°F in the shade, and overnight lows in the 90s. Its own summer guidance is that it does not recommend hiking in the lower elevations in the summer at all, that visitors should stay on paved roads and close to an air-conditioned car, drink at least a gallon of water a day, and stay with the vehicle if it breaks down. Read that as a description of the drive, not a warning to hikers: in July the Death Valley road is a transit through a place you cannot get out of the car in, and the Tonopah road is the better choice. Death Valley's own pleasant seasons are winter and spring, when its January highs average in the high 60s, and those are precisely the months when Tioga Pass is shut. The two parks' seasons are nearly opposite, which is the single most useful fact in planning this trip.

Two windows make both parks work in one drive: late May and June, once the pass has opened and before the desert has fully committed to summer, and October into early November, after the desert has cooled and before the pass closes. Both are gambles at the edges, and the October one depends on a closing date nobody knows in advance. Inside those windows, the Death Valley road is the trip. Outside them, it is either an oven or a dead end.

Practicalities for the Death Valley road, from the park's own pages. Gas is sold at three places inside the park, Furnace Creek, Stovepipe Wells, and Panamint Springs, and the Park Service's warning about relying on a vehicle's navigation system there is in capital letters, because GPS routings have sent drivers onto dead-end and closed roads for years. The park has no staffed entrance stations: the fee is $30 per vehicle for seven days, paid online in advance, at automated machines that take physical cards only, or at the Furnace Creek visitor center and the Stovepipe Wells ranger station in business hours, and the park does not accept cash. An America the Beautiful pass covers it, and covers Yosemite's vehicle fee at the other end, which takes the $80 pass most of the way to paying for itself on a two-park trip before you count anything else.

The pass itself: the last seventy-four miles

From Lee Vining, the Park Service quotes 74 miles and two hours to Yosemite Valley, and the two hours are honest only if you refuse to stop, which nobody does. The road climbs more than 3,000 feet in about twelve miles to the pass, where the Tioga Pass entrance station collects the park fee: $35 per private vehicle, good for seven consecutive days, cards or mobile payment only, no cash. In 2026 every non-US resident aged sixteen and older also pays a $100 non-resident fee on top of the vehicle fee unless the party holds an annual pass; the international visitors page works the arithmetic, including the case where a single $250 non-resident pass covers the car.

Two things about the road east of the pass. There is no gas between Lee Vining and Crane Flat, roughly sixty miles of mountain highway with serious climbing in it, and the old Tuolumne Meadows station that some travel sites still list has been out of operation for years; fill up at Lee Vining, the last pump on the east side. And in the shoulder months the pass is a different place from the Valley: mornings in the 20s and 30s when the Valley is mild, black ice at dawn and dusk near Olmsted Point and on the climb, and chain controls that the park says can be posted as early as September. Carry chains from mid-September on, whatever the forecast says, because the rule inside the park is that every vehicle must have them in possession when controls are in effect, four-wheel drives and rental cars included.

Then the reward. The Tioga Road is not a transit corridor but the high country of the park, and an eastern arrival sees it at its best: Tuolumne Meadows at 8,600 feet, Tenaya Lake against its domes, and Olmsted Point, where Half Dome appears from behind for the first time. A day in Tuolumne Meadows makes the case for giving it the whole day rather than the windshield, and on this trip you have already paid for the drive. Descend to the Valley in the late afternoon, when the day-use cars are leaving.

Where to break the drive in season

The shape that works is to sleep on the east side of the mountains and cross the pass in the morning. Eight hours from Las Vegas puts you at the Valley floor at dusk with nothing seen; six or seven puts you in the Eastern Sierra with the evening to spare and the best road in the park still ahead, in daylight, the next day.

Lee Vining is the natural place to stop, and the gateway towns comparison is candid about what it is: a tiny outpost between Mono Lake and the pass, the only east-side gateway, and no substitute for the western towns as a Valley base. For one night on the way through it is exactly right. Its services are thin and seasonal on top of thin, the Tioga Gas Mart with the Whoa Nellie Deli runs roughly late April to late October, the market is small, and the nearest full supermarket is in Mammoth Lakes, about thirty miles south. Mammoth Lakes and June Lake are the deeper bench of rooms and the sensible fallback when Lee Vining is full, at the cost of thirty to forty minutes added to the morning.

In the two shoulder windows, the other honest overnight is inside Death Valley or at its edge, at Furnace Creek, Stovepipe Wells, or Panamint Springs, which turns the drive into two comfortable days, the desert one afternoon and evening and the whole of the Eastern Sierra and the pass the next. In summer, do not: the park's own guidance is to be in an air-conditioned car and moving.

The east-side night, on the way

A Lee Vining bed means crossing Tioga Pass in the morning with the high country ahead of you in daylight instead of behind you in the dark. Rooms are few and seasonal; when the town is full, Mammoth Lakes and June Lake are the next bench, half an hour south.

The winter road: around the bottom of the mountains

When the pass is closed, the Park Service's published detour from Las Vegas is I-15 south to Barstow, Highway 58 west to Bakersfield, Highway 99 north to Fresno, and Highway 41 north into the park: 495 miles and eight to ten hours. There is no shortcut. The other high passes north of Tioga close for the winter as well, and the alternative the Park Service publishes for the Eastern Sierra towns, north through Minden and over US 50, is the wrong direction from Nevada's south end. From Bakersfield on, this is the Los Angeles drive, and everything in that piece from Highway 99 north applies: the tule fog on the Valley floor in the wet months, the climb through Oakhurst and Fish Camp, and the arrival at Tunnel View that the southern approach alone delivers.

Three things change in winter beyond the mileage. Chains. The park says chains are most often required from November through March, sometimes as early as September or as late as May, and most often on the higher roads, the Wawona Road from the South Entrance among them. All vehicles must carry them when controls are posted, four-wheel drive and rentals included, and the fine for not having them runs to several thousand dollars. The winter guide explains the R1, R2, and R3 levels and why Highway 140 from Merced is the lowest and most reliable road when a storm is on; from Bakersfield that means staying on 99 past Fresno to Merced and coming in through Mariposa, a longer drive but the road least likely to be turned around. Daylight. Ten hours of driving in December is most of the daylight there is, which is the strongest argument for the Bakersfield or Oakhurst night. The Grapevine does not apply: the Barstow road joins 99 at Bakersfield without crossing Tejon Pass, though Highway 58 does cross the Tehachapi Mountains, so check it with everything else.

There is a better winter shape than the straight run, and the Park Service's own detour table contains it. Its Death Valley detour, Highway 190 west to US 395, south to Highway 14, Highway 58 to Bakersfield, then 99 and 41: 450 miles and about nine hours, is the route from the middle of Death Valley, not from Las Vegas, and that is the point. Winter is Death Valley's season. Drive from Las Vegas into the desert the first day, sleep there, and take the detour road to Oakhurst or Fish Camp the second, and the winter trip stops being a penalty and becomes a two-park drive in the months when the desert is at its best. The pass you cannot cross is the price; the desert in January is what it buys.

The loop, and the question of which way round

Most people driving from Las Vegas are not driving back to Las Vegas the same way, and in season the trip is best planned as a loop: in over Tioga Pass, out through the South Entrance and down to Bakersfield and Barstow, which is the winter road driven in the other direction. Do it that way round rather than the reverse. Arriving from the east puts the high country first, when you are fresh and the light is on the domes, and saves the Valley for the middle of the trip; leaving by the south puts the Mariposa Grove on the way out, a quarter mile inside the gate, and the Tunnel View stop on the last morning. The reverse loop spends the fresh day on the flat and crosses the pass tired, in the afternoon, with the Eastern Sierra descent in the dark. If the return is to San Francisco instead, the same logic holds, in from the east and out by the west, and the entrances guide lays out the western roads.

Without a car

Mostly this trip does not work without one, and it is better to say so plainly. There is no scheduled public bus from Las Vegas to the park that I know of. What does exist is the last leg: in summer, YARTS runs a Highway 395 bus from Mammoth Lakes through June Lake and Lee Vining, over the pass to Tuolumne Meadows, White Wolf, Crane Flat, and Yosemite Valley. The 2026 season is July 1 through September 30 for the main runs, with a published adult fare of $30 one way from Mammoth Lakes to the Valley, $16 to Tuolumne Meadows, and $6 from Lee Vining to Tuolumne; reservations are sold online in limited numbers and walk-ons ride as space permits. It solves the mountain crossing for anyone who can reach Mammoth Lakes by other means, and the shuttle and YARTS guide covers the rest of the system. Commercial tour operators sell multi-day Yosemite bus trips out of Las Vegas; I have not taken one, but they exist, and they are the honest answer for a visitor with no licence.

The shape of the trip that works

In season, three nights. Leave Las Vegas after breakfast on the Tonopah road, or on the Death Valley road in the two shoulder windows, and sleep in Lee Vining or Mammoth Lakes. Cross Tioga Pass early, spend the day between Tuolumne Meadows, Tenaya Lake, and Olmsted Point, and drop into the Valley for the evening. Give the Valley the whole of the next day. On the fourth morning leave by the South Entrance, walk the Mariposa Grove while it is still cool, stop at Tunnel View on the way past, and drive the long southern road home or on to the coast.

In winter, the same three nights with the geography reversed. Las Vegas to Death Valley the first day, and a night in the desert while it is mild. Death Valley to Oakhurst or Fish Camp the second, by 395, 14, 58, 99, and 41, chains in the trunk. Into the park early on the third morning, Tunnel View and the Valley, and a second Valley night for the winter light. The high country is not on this version, because it is under snow, and the Valley in winter is a better consolation than most first-time visitors expect.

Either way, three checks before you leave the hotel: the pass, on the park's road line; the desert forecast, because 116°F changes what a breakdown means; and the chain rule, if the month is anywhere between September and May. The planning guide will build the read list for your dates, and it knows which months close which roads.
