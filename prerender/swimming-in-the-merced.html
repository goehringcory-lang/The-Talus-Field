On a hot August afternoon the Merced River is the best thing in Yosemite Valley, and it is not close. Green water over pale granite, black oaks throwing shade onto sand, El Capitan standing over the whole business, and a temperature drop of twenty degrees the moment you get in. It is also the setting for a steady run of rescues and drownings, year after year, and the Park Service pulls fifteen to twenty people out of the water in a normal season. Both of those sentences are true about the same river. The distance between them is mostly the calendar, and secondarily about knowing the rules.

The season is the safety rule

The Merced is a snowmelt river. It does not warm up gradually and evenly the way a lowland river does; it runs high, fast and near freezing through the spring melt, then drops over a few weeks into something entirely different. The same stretch of water that will drown a strong adult in May is a place where six-year-olds stand around in ankle-deep water in August.

The practical version, and this is the one the site has taken as its position for years: do not swim in the Merced in spring. Through the melt the current is stronger than it looks, the water is cold enough to disable you in under a minute, and spring is when people get carried off the bank. By high summer, once the flow has dropped and the water has had weeks of sun on shallow granite, it becomes the family swimming river everybody pictures. By September it is low, warm and slow.

As a rough guide: the swimming season runs from about mid-July into September, and in June the answer is almost always not yet. But the transition does not happen on a date, and I am not going to pretend otherwise, because it moves several weeks in either direction with the snowpack. In a heavy year the river can still be running spring volumes well into July. What you actually want is not a date at all: it is the flow on the day you are standing there.

How to check before you get in

There is a gauge on the Merced at Pohono Bridge, at the west end of Yosemite Valley, and it is the number the park itself regulates against. The park's own boating rule keys off it: when the Pohono Bridge gauge reads above four feet at eight in the morning, flotation is mandatory for everyone on the water. That threshold is a useful signal even if you have no intention of getting into a boat. If the river is high enough that the Park Service has made life jackets compulsory for rafters, it is not a swimming day, and no amount of the water looking calm at your particular beach changes that.

Check current river conditions and any posted closures on the park's conditions page before you go, and look at the river itself when you arrive. Water moving fast enough to push a stick along faster than you would walk is water that will move you too.

Cold water is what actually gets people

The mental model most visitors carry is hypothermia: cold water is dangerous because you slowly get too cold. That model is wrong in a way that kills strong swimmers, because the dangerous part happens in the first minute, not the first hour.

Going into cold water triggers an involuntary gasp, followed by breathing you cannot control. If your head is under at the moment of the gasp, you inhale water. If it is not, you spend the next half minute or so hyperventilating and unable to hold a breath, which is exactly when a current is deciding where you go. Then, over the following minutes, your hands and forearms stop doing what you ask of them, well before you feel dangerously cold. People do not drown in snowmelt because they are bad swimmers. They drown because the ability to swim is temporarily removed from them.

The behavioral consequences are worth stating plainly. Get in gradually rather than jumping, so the gasp happens while you are standing up in shallow water. Do not enter cold water alone. Do not enter it upstream of anything you would not want to be swept into. And treat the first thirty seconds as the hazard, rather than the swim.

Where people swim

Swimming is permitted in the Merced through Yosemite Valley, and there are no lifeguards anywhere on it. There is no designated swimming area and no supervised beach. What there are, instead, are a handful of places where the river has laid down sand and slowed enough to be sensible.

Sentinel Beach is the default: a sandy bank on the south side with a picnic area attached, calm by late summer, and Half Dome visible from the water. It is the one to take small children to.

Cathedral Beach, downstream, is wider and deeper, with El Capitan filling the view upstream. Better for adults who want to actually swim, and worth more caution earlier in the season because the current through it is stronger than Sentinel's.

Devil's Elbow is a bend on Northside Drive that turns into a good swimming reach once the river drops. The Swinging Bridge area, on Southside Drive with Upper Yosemite Fall in the view, is shallow and sandy and the best wading spot in the Valley for toddlers. Jumping or diving from the bridge itself is not permitted. Note also that there are two Swinging Bridges in this park: this one, and a different one down in Wawona.

Wawona, on the South Fork of the Merced at 4,000 feet with a much smaller drainage above it, has the warmest swimming in the park and is worth the drive on a heat-wave afternoon. Tenaya Lake, at 8,150 feet on Tioga Road, is the opposite proposition: a real sand beach and genuinely cold water, covered in the Tuolumne day guide.

Where you must not, which is not a matter of judgment

Swimming is prohibited, not discouraged, in a specific list of places. Above Vernal Fall, that means Emerald Pool and the Silver Apron. It also means Hetch Hetchy Reservoir and the first mile up its tributaries, the Dana Fork of the Tuolumne, Lake Eleanor where posted, and the Wawona domestic water intake and the hundred yards above it.

The Emerald Pool closure is the one people argue with, because it is a beautiful green pool at the top of a hard climb on a hot day, and the water above it looks flat. The Park Service has documented what happens there. In one afternoon, five teenagers were injured sliding down the closed Silver Apron. On another, two eighteen-year-olds swimming in the Emerald Pool were caught by a current they had not seen in water cold enough to take their breath, and one of them was pulled out by a passing stranger. Those are the incidents where everyone lived.

In July 2011 three visitors in their twenties, Hormiz David, Ninos Yacoub and Ramina Badal, climbed over the metal guardrail about twenty-five feet above the brink of Vernal Fall, after other hikers had called out to them not to. All three went over. The recoveries took months. The Mist Trail guide makes the same point from the trail's side, and it is the single rule on that trail that has cost the most lives: the water above a waterfall is moving faster than it appears, the granite under it is polished and frictionless, and there is no version of losing your footing there that you recover from.

Never swim above a waterfall. Not at the top of Vernal Fall, not at the top of Nevada Fall, not in any pool that drains toward an edge you cannot see over.

Rafting, and the flotation rule

The Valley's classic float runs from Stoneman Bridge down to Sentinel Beach, a slow three miles through the middle of the Valley, and it may be the single best way to spend a ninety-degree afternoon in Yosemite. Rafts are rented in season at Curry Village, with a shuttle back. Season, price and age or weight minimums move year to year, so check current-season details rather than trusting a number you read somewhere, including here.

If you bring your own inflatable, the rules that matter are these. Boating is restricted above Little Yosemite Valley and below El Capitan Bridge. Children aged thirteen and under must wear a life jacket on the water at all times. And when the Pohono Bridge gauge is above four feet at eight in the morning, everybody wears one, regardless of age or competence. A pool inflatable is not a raft, a river is not a lake, and the Merced has moved plenty of people off cheap flotation and into the water.

The hazards that are not the cold

Strainers. A submerged log or a fallen branch lets water through and does not let you through. This is the classic river killer and it is invisible from upstream. Never swim toward or under downed timber in moving water.

Foot entrapment. Standing up in moving water deeper than your knees is how a foot gets wedged between rocks and the current puts you face down. If you are being carried, float on your back with your feet up and pointing downstream, and do not try to stand until you are out of the current.

Slick rock. Granite at the waterline grows a film of algae that is far more slippery than it looks, and most Merced injuries are not drownings at all. They are people going down hard on rock. Water shoes are not overkill here; the riverbed is cobble and the banks are polished stone.

Water that looks slower than it is. A smooth surface means depth, not calm. The fastest water in a river is usually the water making the least noise.

Getting in without wrecking the bank

The Park Service asks visitors to enter and leave the river only at sandy beaches, and this is not merely an erosion-control nicety. The Merced through the Valley is a protected Wild and Scenic river, and the few miles of riverbank through the developed part of the Valley take an enormous amount of foot traffic. Banks that get scrambled up and down collapse, the vegetation that holds them goes, and the beach itself eventually goes with it. Sections are fenced and revegetating for exactly this reason.

So: walk to the sand, get in from the sand, get out at the sand. It also happens to be the safest way in, which is not a coincidence. The places where the bank is intact and steep are the places where the current is doing the work.

With children

Pick Sentinel Beach or the Swinging Bridge shallows and stay there. Put small children in life jackets even when they are only standing in ankle-deep water, because the transition from ankle-deep to over-their-head can be one step on a shelving sandbar. Water shoes for everyone. Assign one adult to watch the water and nothing else, and rotate that job, because drowning is famously quiet and a group of adults who are all half-watching is a group where nobody is watching. The guide to Yosemite with kids has more on structuring a Valley day around a river afternoon rather than treating it as the thing you do if there is time left, which is the right way around.

The short version

  1. Do not swim during the spring melt. High summer into September is the season, and the changeover moves with the snowpack rather than the calendar.
  2. If the Pohono Bridge gauge is above four feet, the park requires life jackets on the water. Treat that as the river telling you it is not a swimming day.
  3. Cold water disables you in the first minute, before hypothermia is anywhere in the picture. Get in slowly, never alone.
  4. Swim at the sandy beaches: Sentinel, Cathedral, Devil's Elbow, Swinging Bridge. There are no lifeguards on the river.
  5. Swimming is prohibited above Vernal Fall, at Emerald Pool and the Silver Apron, and in the several other places the park lists. That is a rule, not advice.
  6. Never swim above any waterfall, anywhere, at any flow.
  7. Enter and exit only at sandy beaches, for the bank's sake and for yours.
  8. Wawona is warmer, Tenaya Lake is colder, and both are worth the drive.

None of this is an argument against getting in the water. It is an argument against getting in the water the way most people do it: with no plan at all, in flip-flops, on the hottest afternoon of the year, having driven four hours and wanting to be cold. An hour on a sandbar with cold water on your legs and a wall of granite going gold above you is one of the great cheap pleasures available in a national park. It costs almost nothing to take it seriously enough to keep it that way.
