A lot of people who would love Yosemite never go because they think you have to hike to "really" see it. They're not wrong about hiking being part of the experience for many visitors. They are wrong that hiking is the only way in. The truth is that Yosemite National Park is built for non-hikers more thoroughly than almost any other national park in the country, and a non-hiker can have a complete, satisfying, wonder-filled visit without ever putting on hiking boots. I've watched this happen hundreds of times. I've watched grandparents in their eighties have what they describe as the best trip of their lives without walking more than half a mile in a day. I've watched parents with toddlers see things in Yosemite that the toddlers will remember twenty years later.

This guide is for that audience. The non-hikers. Which includes:

  • Seniors and people with mobility limits.
  • Visitors with strollers and very young children.
  • People recovering from injury or surgery.
  • People who just don't like hiking. (This is allowed. It is a reasonable life choice.)
  • People with very limited time who want the highlights.
  • People with respiratory or cardiac conditions who can't do exertion.
  • Wheelchair users. The Park Service publishes a detailed accessibility guide, linked at the bottom of this piece; this article complements it, it does not replace it.

What follows is what I would tell a friend who fits any of those descriptions and is asking me whether they should go to Yosemite at all.

The answer is yes. Here's what to do when you get there.

Why Yosemite is built for this

Some quick context that matters.

Yosemite Valley sits at about 4,000 feet in a granite trough roughly a mile wide and seven miles long. The roads that loop around the Valley pass within view of, or directly under, almost every iconic feature in the park. Tunnel View, El Capitan, Bridalveil Fall, Cathedral Rocks, Cook's Meadow, Yosemite Falls, Half Dome, Sentinel Rock, the Royal Arches can all be seen from a car. Many can be seen from a car without getting out. Several can be photographed from the parking lot.

The free shuttle system, when it is running, connects most of the major points within the Valley, and every bus in the fleet carries a wheelchair lift and tie-downs. The drive up to Glacier Point ends at a parking lot a few hundred yards from one of the great viewpoints in any national park anywhere. The drive on Tioga Road through the high country is itself one of the great scenic drives in the United States, with major stops accessible from the road shoulder.

A non-hiker isn't getting a watered-down Yosemite. They're getting Yosemite as the road system was designed to deliver it. The trails are an addition, not the substitute.

A word about the word "accessible"

This article uses two different words on purpose, and the difference is not pedantry.

Paved means the surface is asphalt or boardwalk rather than dirt and rock. It says nothing about grade. Several paved trails in Yosemite climb hard enough that a manual wheelchair user cannot self-propel them and a person with a cardiac limit should not attempt them. Bridalveil Fall is the classic example: the trail is paved end to end, and the Park Service still does not describe the full length as wheelchair accessible, because of the grade.

Wheelchair accessible is the Park Service's own designation, and in Yosemite it is applied narrowly and honestly. The routes that carry it in the Valley are short and genuinely flat: the Cook's Meadow loop, the eastern leg of the Lower Yosemite Fall trail, the Big Trees Loop in Mariposa Grove, the path from the Glacier Point lot to the overlook.

Where I know which label applies, I say so below. Where a surface is merely paved and not flat, I say that instead. If you are planning around a wheelchair, a walker, or a heart condition, treat "paved" as a warning rather than a promise, and read the NPS accessibility guide before you commit to anything.

Before you go: the logistics that actually matter

Reservations. There is no day-use or peak-hours entry reservation for the 2026 season. You drive up to the entrance station, pay the entrance fee, and go in. That is a change from 2024 and 2025, and it means the constraint has moved from the gate to the parking lot.

Parking. On busy summer and holiday days, Valley day-use parking is effectively full by mid-morning. For a group that cannot walk far, a full lot is not an inconvenience, it is the end of the plan. Arrive early, or arrive after about four in the afternoon and make sunset the point of the day.

Accessibility placards. Bring yours and hang it. In Yosemite, a placard is not just a parking privilege. It opens roads and pullouts that are otherwise closed to cars, including the drive up Mariposa Grove Road to the Grizzly Giant parking area and, during the February Horsetail Fall event, the closed stretch of Northside Drive and the El Capitan Picnic Area. A placard vehicle can drive to places everyone else is walking a mile and a half to reach.

The free shuttle. Two routes run in Yosemite Valley: the Valleywide Shuttle, which serves the full length of the Valley including lodging, food, and trailheads, and the East Valley Shuttle, which is limited to the eastern end. Both run daily from 7 a.m. to 10 p.m. when in service, and both are lift-equipped with tie-downs (the published wheelchair limit is 24 inches wide by 46 inches long). The service is seasonal, not year-round, and start and end dates move with conditions, so confirm before you build a day around it. Riding the shuttle in a loop, getting off nowhere, is itself a decent low-effort tour of the Valley.

Wheelchair and scooter rental. A limited number of manual wheelchairs and electric scooters rent seasonally from the bike stands at Yosemite Valley Lodge and Curry Village. Limited means limited. Reserve ahead by phone rather than arriving and hoping.

Getting there without driving. YARTS, the regional bus system, runs into the Valley from the gateway towns. Whether the fare also covers the park entrance fee is currently unsettled, with the Park Service and the YARTS fares page saying opposite things, so budget for the gate. The Highway 120 East route over Tioga Pass, connecting Mammoth Lakes and Lee Vining to Tuolumne Meadows and the Valley, runs a short summer season only. The Highway 140 corridor from Merced and Mariposa runs the longest season of the YARTS routes.

The Valley by car: what you actually stop at

First, the one piece of Valley geography that trips people up. The Valley floor is a one-way loop. Southside Drive carries traffic east, deeper into the Valley. Northside Drive carries traffic west, back out. You cannot turn around and go back for a stop you missed without driving the whole loop again, which costs twenty minutes or more in summer traffic. Plan the stops in order.

The itinerary below runs west to east on Southside Drive, then east to west on Northside Drive. It can be done in three to four hours including a stop for lunch, and it includes most of the iconic views in Yosemite Valley.

Tunnel View. If you come in on Highway 41 from Wawona, you pass it on the way down. Parking lots sit on both sides of the road, the view is straight off the pavement, and there are no stairs. What you actually walk: nothing, unless you park on the uphill side, in which case you cross a road with fast traffic and no signal. This is the postcard composition: Bridalveil Fall on the right, El Capitan on the left, Half Dome centered at the back. Give it ten minutes. The light changes it completely.

Bridalveil Fall. The parking area is just past the Wawona Road junction, at the west end of Southside Drive. The trail was rebuilt and reopened in fall 2023 with a new parking area, new boardwalk sections, and new viewing platforms. What you actually walk: about half a mile round trip on pavement and boardwalk, with roughly 80 feet of climb. The Park Service does not call the full length wheelchair accessible, because of that grade. There is a viewing platform partway up that gets you a real view for less of the climb, and it is worth stopping there rather than pushing on. Best in May and June, when the fall is loud and the spray reaches the trail. By late summer it can be a trickle.

Cathedral Beach and Sentinel Beach picnic areas. Both on Southside Drive, both riverside, both with parking within sight of the tables. What you actually walk: from the car to a table, or a short sandy path to the water. These are the best lunch stops in the Valley for a group that does not want to queue in Yosemite Village, and the upstream view of Half Dome from the riverbank is one of the quiet pleasures of the park.

Sentinel Bridge. Where Sentinel Drive crosses the Merced, connecting Southside Drive to the Village. Park in the lot and walk out onto the bridge. What you actually walk: fifty feet of level pavement, no stairs, railings the whole way. The view east from the middle of the bridge, Half Dome standing over the river, is one of the most photographed compositions in Yosemite and the single highest reward-to-effort stop in the Valley.

Cook's Meadow. The loop starts from the Sentinel Drive parking area, and the Park Service lists it as one mile, mostly level, and wheelchair accessible. What you actually walk: as much or as little as you like. The reflected Yosemite Falls view that people come for is available in the first few hundred feet, on boardwalk, and there are benches at intervals if you take the whole loop. This is the stop I send people to when they have one hour and one working knee.

Yosemite Village. Food, restrooms, the Exploration Center, the museum, the gallery, the store. Everything is close together and level. Covered in more detail below.

Lower Yosemite Fall. The trailhead is near Yosemite Valley Lodge, on the Northside Drive side, and it is a shuttle stop. What you actually walk: the full loop is 1.1 miles with about 50 feet of elevation change, roughly half an hour at a normal pace. The eastern leg is paved and wheelchair accessible, and it is the leg that reaches the bridge at the base of the fall. Take the east leg out and back rather than the loop if the loop is too much. In spring the bridge gets wet from spray. By late summer and fall the fall often goes dry, and there is no point pretending otherwise: check before you go.

El Capitan Meadow. Roadside pullouts on Northside Drive with a wide view up the wall. What you actually walk: from the car to the meadow edge, on flat ground, as far as you feel like. Bring binoculars. On a summer afternoon you can pick out climbers as slow white specks and haul bags hanging below them, which is a thing most people have never seen with their own eyes.

Valley View. A riverside pullout at the west end of Northside Drive, sometimes called Gates of the Valley, looking back east into the Valley. What you actually walk: a few dozen yards of flat, mostly firm ground to the riverbank, with boulders and benches to sit on. Bridalveil Fall, El Capitan, and Cathedral Rocks in one frame with the Merced in the foreground. It is the last thing you see on the way out and it is better than Tunnel View in the late afternoon.

Two low-effort day plans

The half day, almost no walking. Tunnel View, ten minutes. Bridalveil Fall parking, and as much of the paved trail as the grade allows, or skip it and look up at the fall from the lot. Lunch at Cathedral Beach or Sentinel Beach picnic area. Sentinel Bridge, fifty feet, for the Half Dome view. Cook's Meadow, first few hundred feet of boardwalk. Out on Northside Drive with a stop at El Capitan Meadow and a last stop at Valley View. Total walking: under half a mile, all of it optional, none of it steep.

The full day, one big view. Morning in the Valley on the loop above, ending with lunch in the Village or at a picnic area. Afternoon drive up to Glacier Point, about an hour each way from the Valley, with the paved path to the overlook and an hour to sit at the railing. Sunset at Glacier Point if you can stay for it, and drive down in the dark. Total walking: the Valley stops plus 300 yards of paved switchback at Glacier Point. If Glacier Point Road is closed, substitute Tunnel View at sunset and do not consider it a downgrade.

The framework under both plans: one drive, one meal sitting down, one big view, and nothing scheduled tightly. The failure mode for this kind of trip is not doing too little, it is stacking three stops onto a group that had energy for two.

Glacier Point and Tioga Road

Both of these are scenic drives that deliver views non-hikers can't get from anywhere else in the park.

Glacier Point Road is open by car from approximately late May through October or November, depending on snow. The drive from the Valley takes about an hour each way. What you actually walk: a 300-yard paved switchback path from the parking lot down to the overlook, which the Park Service does list as wheelchair accessible. It is a descent going out and a climb coming back, so budget more time for the return than the distance suggests. The point stands about 3,200 feet directly above Curry Village on the Valley floor, with Half Dome across the gap at eye level and the high country behind it. There is a snack bar, a gift shop, restrooms, and an amphitheater at the top, all accessible, plus a bronze tactile relief model of the park's geology on the terrace below the gift shop. Rangers give evening programs here in season, and sunset from the railing is the single best hour a non-hiker can spend in Yosemite.

Tioga Road is open from approximately late May or June through October or November, depending on snow. It climbs from Valley elevation to nearly 10,000 feet at Tioga Pass. The drive is the experience, and the elevation is worth flagging: if altitude is a medical issue for anyone in the car, Tuolumne Meadows at 8,600 feet will be felt. A full day in Tuolumne is worth planning properly rather than driving through, and that guide is built around the short walks rather than the summits. Stops worth making, all from the road shoulder or short paths:

  • Olmsted Point. A parking lot view back into the head of Tenaya Canyon, including the back side of Half Dome. The view is available from the lot; the short spur to the lower viewpoint is over bare granite and is not a smooth surface. (At night, it's also the best stargazing pullout in the park.)
  • Tenaya Lake. A subalpine lake with a granite shoreline and several pullouts. The east end has a small beach a short flat walk from the parking area, and it is the easiest place in the high country to simply sit by water.
  • Tuolumne Meadows. A broad alpine meadow at 8,600 feet with pullouts along its length. Most of the meadow views require no walking at all. The Soda Springs path is mostly flat but it is a dirt road surface, not pavement.

If you can't or don't want to drive Tioga Road yourself, there is a summer bus from the Valley up to Tuolumne Meadows, and the YARTS Highway 120 East route covers the same road on a short summer season. Both are real options for a non-driver, and both run on a limited schedule that has to be checked rather than assumed.

Mariposa Grove, which is the best accessible walk in the park

The grove of giant sequoias near the park's south entrance is, for a non-hiker, the most rewarding place in Yosemite that is not a viewpoint. It was rebuilt in 2018 specifically to move parking away from the trees and put visitors on good surfaces.

How it works. You park at the Mariposa Grove Welcome Plaza, near the south entrance, and ride a free shuttle about two miles up to the arrival area and trailhead. Shuttles run roughly every fifteen minutes in season and are wheelchair accessible.

What you actually walk. The Big Trees Loop is a 0.3-mile loop from the arrival area, hard-surfaced and wheelchair accessible, with level boardwalk over the wet ground, benches, and interpretive panels. It passes the Fallen Monarch. It is short, it is flat, and it puts you among mature giant sequoias, which is not a thing you can fake with a viewpoint.

If you have a placard. Vehicles displaying a disability placard may drive Mariposa Grove Road past the shuttle to the Grizzly Giant parking area, from which a short accessible trail reaches the Grizzly Giant itself, the largest tree in the grove. This is the single biggest practical benefit of the placard anywhere in Yosemite.

In winter. Mariposa Grove Road is not plowed and closes to vehicles, roughly December into April, and the shuttle stops with it. The road and the trails stay open to people on foot, snowshoes, and skis, which means the grove in winter is a real walk of several miles round trip from the Welcome Plaza, not a drive-up. For most non-hikers, winter is the season to skip the grove.

Let someone else drive

The park concessioner runs guided bus and tram tours out of Yosemite Valley Lodge, and for a group that does not want to deal with parking, one-way roads, or the driving itself, they solve the whole problem. Book ahead, and say what you need when you book rather than when you arrive.

The Valley Floor Tour is the core one: a two-hour, 26-mile circuit of the Valley with a guide, running year-round. In the warm months it runs on an open-air tram; in the cold months it runs in a heated motor coach with panoramic windows. The open-air tram is boarded with an external ramp, so tell the ticket desk in advance if you need it.

The Glacier Point Tour is a four-hour round trip from the Valley up to Glacier Point and back, running whenever Glacier Point Road is open. It is also the standard way to get to Glacier Point without driving the mountain road yourself.

The Grand Tour is a full day combining the Valley, Glacier Point, and Mariposa Grove, departing Yosemite Valley Lodge in the morning. It runs in an enclosed shuttle with a lift, and seating can be adjusted for a wheelchair if you request it in advance of arrival. For a visitor with one day and no interest in driving, this is the most park you can see from a seat.

A seasonal Tuolumne Meadows tour runs up Tioga Road in summer, and there are naturalist-led evening tram tours in the Valley in the warm months.

Cultural and indoor sites

For variety, or for weather days, or for slower-paced visitors, Yosemite has more indoor and cultural content than people realize. All of it is in Yosemite Village, within a few level minutes of each other.

The Yosemite Exploration Center is the Valley's main visitor center, in Yosemite Village, open year-round from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. It carries the park's interactive exhibits and a Yosemite Conservancy bookstore. If you know it as the Valley Visitor Center, this is the same place under a newer name.

The park film, Spirit of Yosemite, plays in the theater behind the Exploration Center on the hour and the half hour, from mid-morning to late afternoon, year-round. It is captioned and audio described. Twenty-odd minutes in a seat, indoors, is a genuinely useful thing to have in the middle of a day for a group that is pacing itself.

The Yosemite Museum has exhibits on the human and natural history of the park, including a reconstructed Ahwahneechee bark house and demonstrations of traditional crafts when staff are available. Free admission.

The Ansel Adams Gallery is a working photography gallery a short walk from the Exploration Center, with original prints by Adams and work by contemporary Sierra photographers. Free to enter, open daily.

The Pioneer Yosemite History Center at Wawona is an outdoor cluster of historic buildings, a covered bridge, cabins, a stagecoach office, that you can walk through at your own pace on mostly level ground. Open seasonally, and a natural pairing with Mariposa Grove on the same day since both sit at the south end of the park.

The Ahwahnee is open to the public whether or not you are staying there. The Great Lounge has a fireplace, deep chairs, and windows onto the granite, and anyone can sit in it. The bar is casual. The dining room enforces a dress code at dinner and none at breakfast. Afternoon tea has at times been limited to hotel guests, so ask rather than plan around it. The building itself, and its Native American-inspired interior design, is a destination in its own right, and it is the best place in the park to spend an hour doing nothing.

Ranger programs, which are free and mostly sitting down

The Park Service and the park's partners run walks, talks, and evening programs through the season, and a large share of them are stationary: an amphitheater talk, a program in the Exploration Center theater, an evening program at a campground or at Glacier Point. Evening programs typically run several nights a week in summer.

Schedules change constantly and are published in the current Yosemite Guide, which you are handed at the entrance station. Read it in the car before you plan the day. We keep the current edition condensed on the Park Bulletin, including the free-program clock and what is running this week.

What changes by season

Season matters more for a non-hiker than for a hiker, because a non-hiker's itinerary is made almost entirely of roads, and the roads close.

Winter, roughly December through March. Glacier Point Road, Tioga Road, and Mariposa Grove Road are all closed to vehicles. The Valley roads stay open and plowed, and the Valley in winter is quiet, uncrowded, and often spectacular after a storm. Tire chains may be required even on plowed roads, and the Valley shuttle may not be running its full service. This is the season when the indoor list and The Ahwahnee earn their keep. In February, the Horsetail Fall light event brings crowds and a specific access rule: pullouts along a stretch of Northside Drive close to cars, and general visitors park at Yosemite Falls parking and walk about 1.5 miles each way. Vehicles with a disability placard are excepted from that closure. If you cannot walk three miles round trip in the cold at dusk, the placard is the difference between going and not going.

Spring, roughly April through early June. The best season for this kind of trip. Waterfalls run at peak, the meadows green up, the Valley is not yet at summer crowd levels, and the roadside views are at their best. The high-country roads are still closed or just opening. Bridalveil and Lower Yosemite Fall are worth the walk in this window and much less so later.

Summer, roughly late June through August. Everything is open, including Glacier Point Road, Tioga Road, and the Mariposa Grove shuttle. It is also the season when Valley parking fills by mid-morning, the shuttle is crowded, and heat on the Valley floor is real. Start early or start late. The high country is cooler than the Valley by a wide margin and is the better place to spend a hot afternoon.

Fall, roughly September through November. The quietest good season. Waterfalls are low or dry, which takes Lower Yosemite Fall off the list, but the meadows go gold, the light is long, and the crowds thin sharply after Labor Day. Glacier Point Road and Tioga Road usually stay open into October or November and then close on short notice with the first serious storm.

Wildlife and photography from accessible spots

Some of the best wildlife viewing in Yosemite is from places that require minimal walking.

El Capitan Meadow at sunrise. Park, walk to the meadow edge, sit. Bears, deer, and in fall the migrating songbirds all use this area. Bring binoculars.

Cook's Meadow at any time. Birds, deer, and the occasional bear. The boardwalk gives you a flat surface to walk slowly, and there is nothing wrong with going a hundred feet and standing still for twenty minutes. Standing still is how you see things.

The pullouts along Tioga Road in summer. Marmots on the granite, mule deer in the meadows, sometimes black bears at meadow edges.

The Merced River through the Valley. Mergansers, kingfishers, and herons in the warmer months. Best viewed from the bridges or the riverbank picnic areas.

For photography, the iconic views (Tunnel View, Valley View, Sentinel Bridge, Cook's Meadow, Glacier Point) are all reachable without hiking. The best-known Yosemite photographs are mostly taken from these places. A photographer who never takes a trail can produce a complete portfolio.

Multi-generational trips

The most common version of the non-hiker trip is the family trip with a mix of abilities. Here's the framework that I've watched work over and over.

Stay in or near the Valley. Don't try to base in Wawona or Oakhurst if any of your group has limited mobility. The drive in and out adds friction, and the friction compounds across a week.

Build in low-energy mornings and big-vista afternoons. Late starts are fine. Sunset is the best part of the day for everyone.

Plan one or two short walks per day, separated by a long break. A morning at Cook's Meadow, lunch sitting down, an afternoon drive to Glacier Point, dinner. Don't pack the day.

Pick one big "view day." Glacier Point if the road is open. Mariposa Grove if you are staying at the south end. Tunnel View at sunset if neither is available. Make this the highlight everyone gets to share.

Let the hikers in your group take a half-day to themselves. Drop them at the Mist Trail trailhead early and pick them up at noon, or put them on the shuttle and let them find their own way back. Everyone gets what they want, and nobody spends the day being quietly resented.

The takeaway

You do not have to hike to see Yosemite. You probably already knew that, but you may have been told otherwise often enough to wonder. The version of the park that's available from the road, the bridges, the meadow boardwalks, the sequoia loop, and the lobby of The Ahwahnee is the same Yosemite the hikers are seeing. It's just delivered differently.

Drive the Valley loop in the one direction it runs. Stop at Tunnel View, Sentinel Bridge, Cook's Meadow, El Capitan Meadow, and Valley View. If the roads are open, drive to Glacier Point and ride the shuttle into Mariposa Grove. Sit in the Great Lounge with a coffee. Walk as far up the Bridalveil path as the grade allows and no further. Eat lunch at a riverside picnic table.

That's a real visit. That's the park.
