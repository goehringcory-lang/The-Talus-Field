The trip is two weeks out, or four days out, or it started yesterday, and there is a fire in the park. The news has a map with a red shape on it that looks alarmingly close to the word Yosemite. A friend has sent a photograph of an orange sky. The Park Service says the park is open. Those three things are all true at once, and none of them tells you what you actually need to know, which is whether the places you planned to go, the road you planned to drive and the bed you planned to sleep in are on the wrong side of a closure line. This is the article for that question. It is written in the fall of 2026, with the Dome Fire burning above Wawona, but it is about every fire, because fire is, in the park's own words, a natural and essential part of Yosemite, and the way a fire reaches a visitor's trip is much the same each time.

What follows: what "open" means in a Park Service fire notice, three recent fires and the three different shapes they gave a trip, where to read the situation and in what order, why the smoke and the closures are separate problems, what a closed road does to an itinerary, what happens to each kind of reservation, and how to decide between keeping the trip, reshaping it and moving it.

"Open and operating normally" is a sentence with a list after it

The park's Dome Fire updates carry a version of the same line: Yosemite National Park is open and operating normally, with the following exceptions. Readers tend to hear the first half. The second half is the part that matters, because the exceptions are specific and named. On September 15, 2026, the morning the fire was reported at about 100 acres a quarter mile north of Chilnualna Falls, the exception was one trail: the Chilnualna Falls trail and the trails from the top of the falls toward Buena Vista and Crescent Lakes. By the September 21 update the list had grown to the Ostrander Lake Trail and every area and trail south of it, and the country east of Wawona Road from Chinquapin to Wawona. By the release of September 24 it included Glacier Point Road, closed, in the park's words, "due to reduced visibility from smoke and to safely allow firefighting operations."

So the first thing to do with any fire notice is to skip the reassurance and read the list. A closure is named by a trail, a road, or a pair of place names, and each name can be found on a map and compared with your plan. If nothing on your itinerary is on the list, the fire is, for the moment, a smoke question and a watching brief. If something is, you have a decision, and it is usually a smaller one than the red shape on the news map suggested.

The list also moves, in both directions and without much notice. Nine days separated a single closed trail from a closed road to the park's most visited overlook. The park's own line on when that road comes back is that it "will reopen when conditions allow," which is honest and not a date. Plan as though any closure on the list will still be there on your day, and treat a reopening as good luck rather than a schedule.

Three fires, three shapes

The recent record is the best guide to what a fire does to a visit, and it does not have one shape. It has at least three.

The long closure of one area: the Empire Fire, 2017. Lightning started it, and it was discovered on July 31, 2017, one mile east of the Badger Pass ski area and three quarters of a mile south of Glacier Point Road. The park fought it on three sides to protect Bridalveil Creek Campground, the ski area, the Yosemite West subdivision, Wawona Road and Glacier Point Road, and let the east flank burn toward the Illilouette basin, in the update's words "to continue to enhance forest resiliency in a fire adapted ecosystem." On September 12, six weeks after it was found, it stood at 6,000 acres and 55 percent containment, and Glacier Point Road was still closed "until further notice." For a visitor, that fire was one road and its trailheads, for most of a season, and a smoky morning now and then everywhere else.

The closure of the Valley itself: the Ferguson Fire, 2018. This is the case people remember, and it is the rare one. The Ferguson Fire, the same fire that closed Highway 140 below El Portal, forced the park's hand on July 25, 2018, when it closed Yosemite Valley to visitors. By July 31 the closure covered the Valley, Wawona Road, the Mariposa Grove, the Merced Grove, Hetch Hetchy and other areas, for what the park called "continued unhealthy smoke impacts and ongoing firefighting operations." Even then, Big Oak Flat Road to Crane Flat and the whole of the Tioga Road, with its trails, campgrounds and visitor services, stayed open. The Mariposa Grove reopened on August 13 and the Valley at 9 a.m. on August 14, with Wawona Road still closed between Wawona and the Valley for at least another week and "limited hours and limited visitor services" as the park came back. Twenty days without the Valley, in peak summer, and the high country open throughout.

The fire in the backcountry with a road on its edge: the Dome Fire, 2026. Reported September 15 above Wawona, it spread north and northeast into the footprint of the 2017 Empire Fire, where the park's updates describe crews holding the Bridalveil Creek and Ostrander Lake Trails as the primary contingency line. Cal Fire's incident page, which then listed the cause as under investigation, put it at 5,084 acres and 30 percent contained on the afternoon of September 27, with 781 personnel on it in the park's update of September 25. On September 30 the local press reported that the Forest Service has found the fire human-caused, sparked by an abandoned campfire; the federal incident page still lists the cause as undetermined. Wawona Road stayed open throughout, as did the Valley. What closed was a set of Wawona trails, the backcountry south of Ostrander, and Glacier Point Road.

The pattern across the three is the useful part. A fire in Yosemite usually takes an area, not the park: a road and its trailheads, a drainage and its trails. The whole-Valley closure of 2018 happened, and it could happen again, but it is the exception, and even that one left the Tioga Road open. Plan for the area, and keep the Valley-closure case in mind as the one that would genuinely end a trip.

Where to read it, in order

There is no shortage of information during a fire in the park. The skill is reading it in the right order, from the source that decides to the ones that describe.

  1. The park's current conditions page (nps.gov/yose, Plan Your Visit, Current Conditions). This is the list. Road status, trail closures, which campgrounds are open and which are closed, and the fire restrictions in force. If a place is closed, it is on this page.
  2. The fire's own updates, published in the park's news releases, usually each morning during an active fire. They carry the size, the containment, the day's plan, the full closure list, and a smoke forecast by area. The park's fire news page links them.
  3. The incident page on InciWeb, the interagency incident site, and the public information map the park links from its updates. The map is where the closure lines are drawn against the roads and trails, which is the comparison you actually need to make.
  4. fire.airnow.gov for the air, monitor by monitor. This is a separate question from the closures, and the next section is about it.
  5. The park's phone line, 209/372-0200, for road status and anything the pages have not caught up with.

Social media and news coverage come last, and only as a prompt to check the first two. A news map shows the whole fire in red at a scale where the Valley and Wawona look like neighbors. They are about an hour apart by a winding road, and a fire above one can leave the other untouched. This site's Park Bulletin condenses the current closures on one page, and the conditions board carries the live entrance waits and lot counts, which will tell you on the day how the traffic is shifting around a closed road.

The smoke is a different problem from the fire

A closure is a line on a map. Smoke has no line. It follows the drainages, settles in the low places overnight, and lifts as the day warms, and it reaches areas the fire itself never will. The Dome Fire's smoke forecasts show both halves of that. The update released September 24 expected periods of air unhealthy for sensitive groups in Wawona and Yosemite Valley in the morning, clearing in the afternoon, and good air in Tuolumne Meadows in the morning with unhealthy-for-sensitive-groups periods later in the day. The next morning's update forecast the same rating for Wawona, the Valley and Tuolumne Meadows, and good air at Hodgdon Meadow and in Mariposa.

Two lessons follow. First, the worst of the smoke in the lower park tends to be in the morning, so a smoky dawn is not a verdict on the day; check again at noon. Second, which part of the park has the clean air changes from day to day and is not always the high country. The rule of thumb that Tuolumne sits above the Valley's smoke is often right, and the smoke-season guide lays out when it is and the AQI thresholds for changing plans, but the Dome Fire's forecasts had Tuolumne under the same rating as the Valley on some days. Read the day's forecast and the monitors, not the rule.

Smoke is also the part of a fire that falls unevenly on visitors. A healthy adult on a Valley walk on a sensitive-groups morning is making a different decision from a family with a small child, anyone with asthma or a heart condition, or anyone planning a long, hard climb, which is when a person breathes the most air. Those are the trips where the smoke forecast, and not the closure list, decides the day.

What a closed road does to an itinerary

A road closure closes more than the road. Glacier Point Road is the clearest example, because it carries a string of trailheads and a campground as well as the point itself. The Glacier Point guide walks the road mile by mile: Badger Pass, the McGurk Meadow trailhead, the turn for Bridalveil Creek Campground, the shared Sentinel Dome and Taft Point trailhead, Washburn Point, and the point at the end, with the Ostrander Lake and Mono Meadow trailheads along the way. Close the road and every one of those is out of reach by car, along with the Glacier Point Tour, which drives the same pavement. If your plan had a Sentinel Dome sunset or a Taft Point walk in it, the closure takes it.

It does not take the view. During the Dome Fire the park said explicitly that the Four Mile Trail and the Panorama Trail, which reach Glacier Point from the Valley, would remain open to hikers. That is a real hike, not a substitute for a drive, and the Four Mile and Panorama piece has the climb and the day it takes. With the road closed there is no bus at the top to carry you back down, so either trail becomes an out-and-back from the Valley floor. For a fit party it is arguably the better way to see the place anyway. For a party that planned to drive up, the honest substitute is a different viewpoint: Tunnel View, on Wawona Road at the Valley's west end, and Olmsted Point, on the Tioga Road, are both roadside.

Trail closures work the same way at a smaller scale. A closed trail has a closed trailhead, and sometimes a closed parking area. In Wawona during the Dome Fire, the Chilnualna Falls trail was the one that went first. The rest of Wawona did not, and neither did the Mariposa Grove, which does not appear on the closure lists in the park's Dome Fire updates of that week. The comparison to make is always the same: your specific trailhead against the park's specific list.

What happens to the reservations

This is the part most people are actually asking about, and the answer depends on who holds the booking and whether the park or you are the one who cancels it.

Campgrounds booked on Recreation.gov. The site's policy covers the case where the park closes a facility: "In the event of an emergency closure, the Recreation.gov team or facility manager will refund all fees and will attempt to notify you using the contact information within your Recreation.gov customer profile." Make sure that contact information is current. The harder case is the campground that stays open while you would rather not go. Then the standard cancellation terms apply: a $10 service fee withheld from any refund, and a cancellation the day before or the day of arrival also forfeits the first night's fee. During the Dome Fire the Wawona Campground stayed on the park's open list, and so did every Valley campground. A campground being near a fire does not make it closed, and it does not make its cancellation free.

Wilderness permits. The park's wilderness FAQ is plain that both the application fee and the reservation fee are non-refundable. What it does allow is a change: once a reservation is confirmed, you may adjust the entry date, the group size and the starting trailhead if space is available. If a fire closes your trailhead, a change of trailhead on the other side of the park is the move to try first, and the wilderness center staff can tell you what is open. The permit rules are in the wilderness permits guide; a closed trailhead only changes which one you enter from.

In-park lodging through Yosemite Hospitality. The concessioner's published policy asks for a deposit of the first night at booking and says cancellations or changes "must be received 7 days prior to reservation arrival date to be eligible for a refund." Its published policies page carries no separate clause for fire or park closures. If the park closes the area your room is in, call the reservations line and ask what they are offering; if it has not, the seven-day rule is the one on your confirmation, and it is worth knowing before the fire's next update rather than after.

Gateway-town rooms and rentals. Each has its own terms, and none of them is bound by what the park does. The useful question here is geographic: does the fire, or the smoke, or a closed road, change how you get from this bed to the places you are going? A Groveland room during a fire above Wawona is barely touched. A Wawona or Fish Camp room during the same fire is next to it, and whether that matters depends on the day's smoke and your plans, not the headline.

Keep, reshape, move, or cancel

Four answers, and most fire weeks call for one of the first two.

Keep the trip as planned when nothing on your itinerary is on the closure list and your party is not sensitive to smoke. Check the conditions page the night before and the smoke forecast that morning, and go.

Reshape the days when one piece of the plan is behind a closure. This is the usual case. Swap the closed road for the open one: if Glacier Point is shut, the Tioga Road day is the obvious trade while that road is open, and the Tuolumne day guide is built for it. Move the long hikes to the afternoons if the smoke is a morning problem, or to the part of the park the day's forecast favors. Keep the Valley walks, which are short enough to take on a hazy morning and drop without regret.

Move the base when the smoke is settling on your bed every night, or a closure has cut your lodging off from the rest of your plan. The same question the Highway 140 closure guide asks applies here: which side of the line is your bed on? A fire in the southern park puts Groveland and the Big Oak Flat side at the far end of the drive from it, and the gateway-town comparison covers the trade-offs of each town. Whether a move is possible depends on your current booking's terms and on what is open on your dates on the other side,.

Cancel or postpone in three cases. When the park has closed the area that was the reason for the trip, and a substitute would not be the same trip. When the smoke forecast for your days is at levels your party should not be exercising in, and the smoke-season guide's thresholds give you the numbers. And when the park closes the Valley, as it did in 2018, because at that point every plan built around it has gone. Cancel with the terms in front of you: the Recreation.gov refund for an emergency closure is automatic, and a cancellation of your own is not.

The fire restrictions come with the season, not the fire

One more thing changes during a fire, though the fire did not cause it. In the dry months the park sets fire restrictions, and in September 2026 they applied below 8,000 feet in addition to the park's normal fire rules. They govern campfires and stoves in campgrounds and the backcountry, they are in force whether or not a fire is burning, and a visitor planning a campfire on a fire week should expect them. The fire restrictions guide explains what the stages allow and how to check the one in force.

The practical version

When there is a fire in Yosemite during your trip, read the park's closure list before you read anything else, and compare it name by name with your plan. Treat every closure as lasting through your dates and every reopening as a bonus. Check the smoke forecast by area on the morning of each day, remember that the morning is usually the worst of it in the lower park, and move the long hikes accordingly. Trade a closed road for an open one rather than a trip for no trip. Know your bookings' terms before the next update, not after: the park's emergency closures refund campsites in full, wilderness fees do not come back but trailheads can change, and a room's seven days are seven days. And keep the 2018 case in mind for what it is, the one of these three fires that closed the Valley, and a reminder that even then the Tioga Road stayed open.
