Most people arrive at Clouds Rest by way of a refusal. The Half Dome lottery said no in March, or it said no again two days before the hike, or the trip landed in the weeks after the cables came down, and somebody at a trailhead or on a forum said the same sentence: do Clouds Rest instead, it is higher and there is no permit. That sentence is true, and it undersells the mountain. Clouds Rest is a 9,926-foot summit reached by a maintained trail from Tioga Road, no lottery and no cables, ending on a granite spine with the whole park arranged around it and Half Dome sitting below you. It is the best big summit day in Yosemite that you can simply decide to do. This is how it goes, and when.

The site has recommended this hike before, in passing, inside the Half Dome piece and in the monthly notes. It has never had the guide the recommendation deserves. What follows is the trail from the trailhead the Park Service measures from, the season it lives in, the overnight version, and the few weeks each autumn when it becomes the only summit of its kind still open.

The numbers, from the Sunrise Lakes trailhead

The standard day starts at the Sunrise Lakes trailhead at the west end of Tenaya Lake on Tioga Road, a little over 8,000 feet up and about a mile and a half east of Olmsted Point. The Park Service's figures for the round trip to the summit are 14 miles, 2,300 feet of elevation gain, and eight to ten hours. Both of those last numbers deserve a note. The gain is not one climb: the trail goes up, comes back down, and goes up again, so the day carries more climbing than the net suggests, and the time estimate is honest for a fit party moving steadily with a real stop on top.

For comparison, the Half Dome day from Happy Isles is 14 to 16 miles with 4,800 feet of gain, starting on the Valley floor at about 4,000 feet, and it needs a permit whenever the cables are up. Clouds Rest starts twice as high, climbs half as much, and needs nothing but a parking space. Its summit is more than a thousand feet higher than Half Dome's.

The same trailhead serves the shorter Sunrise Lakes walk, 10.4 miles round trip and 1,300 feet of gain, which shares the first climb with Clouds Rest and then turns off toward the lakes and the High Sierra Camp. It is the fallback if the day turns, and a fine hike in its own right.

The route, in the order it comes at you

The creek. Tenaya Creek crosses the trail within the first few hundred feet. In a heavy snow year it is a cold, knee-deep ford in June; by midsummer it is a rock-hop, and by the end of a dry summer it can be nearly dry. If you are here in early season, carry something to cross in.

The first climb. After a gentle mile and a half through lodgepole forest and over granite slabs, the trail turns uphill and gains roughly a thousand feet in a run of switchbacks to the ridge above Tenaya Canyon. This is the hardest sustained stretch of the day and it comes early, at altitude, on cold legs. Old stone steps and retaining walls mark the way. Take it at a pace you can hold.

The junction and the meadow. At the top of the climb the Sunrise Lakes trail branches off toward the lakes; Clouds Rest continues on the other fork. The trail then loses a few hundred feet into a quiet forested basin with a small pond and a meadow that can be marshy in early season. It is the emptiest part of the day and the last place you will see reliable water.

The second climb. The trail turns toward the summit ridge and begins the second ascent, steady rather than steep, with the trees thinning as it goes. The first views down into Tenaya Canyon arrive here, and they make the case for the rest of the hike.

The ridge. Then the forest ends. The last stretch to the summit follows a narrow granite ridgeline, the spine of the mountain, with Tenaya Canyon dropping away on one side and the Merced drainage on the other. The Park Service describes it in one line and adds one caution: stay off the summit and the ridgeline during thunderstorms or when the rock is wet. It is walking, not climbing. No ropes, no scrambling on the main line, and the granite grips well when dry. But the exposure is real, it is worse in wind, and anyone who dislikes heights will feel it. An older, less maintained path skirts below the crest; the site's Half Dome piece describes it, and it is easy to lose. Most people walk the spine.

The summit. The top is a long, narrow platform, big enough to sit on and eat lunch. The Park Service's description is a 360-degree view of Tenaya Canyon, Yosemite Valley, and countless high Sierra peaks, and for once the official prose is not overselling. Half Dome is south of you and below you, the Valley is laid out to the west, the Cathedral Range and the high country to the east, the Clark Range to the south. You have been looking up at Half Dome all week. This is the place where you look down at it.

Water, altitude, and the summit before noon

Water. Reliable water is in the first miles only: the creek at the trailhead and the seasonal streams and pond in the basin, which can be dry by late summer. From the second climb to the summit and back there is nothing. Carry three liters as a floor and four on a hot day. A filter is worth its weight early in the season and dead weight by September.

Altitude. The entire day is spent between 8,000 and 10,000 feet. A party arriving from sea level and starting this hike the morning after driving up will feel it as a headache, a shortness of breath on the first climb, and a general slowness that the mileage does not explain. Sleep a night at Tuolumne or in the high country first if you can, drink before you are thirsty, eat before you are hungry, and treat a headache with nausea as a reason to descend, not to push.

Lightning. This is the hazard the ridge was built for. The Park Service's guidance for Yosemite is that thunderstorms are most common June through September, that hikers above the treeline or along a rim should plan to be there in the morning and to summit before noon, and that after a storm you should stay in a safe place until 30 minutes after the last thunder. On Clouds Rest, that means a trailhead start at or before first light in summer and a firm turnaround rule: if cloud is building over the crest by late morning, the summit waits for another day. There is no shelter on the spine and no fast way off it.

Wet rock. The same caution covers rain that has already fallen. Granite that grips like sandpaper when dry is a different surface when wet, and the ridge is not a place to learn that.

What to wear and carry. Footwear with real traction, layers for a summit that is often 15 to 20 degrees cooler than the Valley and windy on a day that felt still below, sun protection for the exposed upper half, and the day-pack basics. Bears use these trails; secure your food at every stop, and read the bear piece if you are new to the park's rules.

Getting to the trailhead: you are driving

The trailhead is reachable by vehicle only while Tioga Road is open, which the Park Service puts at typically late May or early June until sometime in October or November; the road closes for the season after the first significant snowfall. In 2026 the road opened May 15. From Yosemite Valley the drive up to Tenaya Lake is well over an hour, and the Tuolumne piece covers what else is up there for the non-hikers in the car.

Parking at the trailhead is limited, a small lot with vault toilets and food lockers, and it fills early on summer weekends. Arrive early or expect to walk in from the shoulder where parking is permitted; check the signs, because not every stretch of shoulder is. The early start you want for the lightning rule solves the parking problem at the same time.

Do not plan on a bus. The free Tuolumne Meadows shuttle and the hikers bus from the Valley are summer services that end in mid-September; in 2026 both stopped on September 13. The YARTS Highway 395/120 East route runs from July 1 through September 30 in 2026 and stops at the Tuolumne Meadows store and visitor center, White Wolf, Crane Flat and Yosemite Village, but not at Tenaya Lake. For most of the season, and for all of the autumn window below, the Sunrise Lakes trailhead is a place you drive to.

Olmsted Point, about a mile and a half back toward the Valley at roughly 8,400 feet, is where you look at the mountain before you climb it: Clouds Rest fills the view up Tenaya Canyon with Half Dome over its shoulder, and a quarter-mile trail out onto the point walks you through the glacial erratics the ice left there. Ten minutes at the rail on the way in, and you will recognize every stage of the trail from above.

The autumn window: between the cables and the snow

Here is the part of the calendar that makes this hike more than a Half Dome consolation prize. Half Dome's cables come down, in the Park Service's standing rule, the day after the second Monday in October, conditions permitting; in 2026 that is October 13, and once they are down the summit is a mountaineering objective, not a hike. Tioga Road, meanwhile, stays open until the first significant snowfall, which in most years is weeks later. Clouds Rest lives in the gap.

The park's own historical dates say how wide the gap has been. The cables have come down between October 10 and October 16 in nine of the last ten seasons the park's table records, and as late as October 22 in 2025. Tioga Road closed for the season on November 1 in 2015, November 16 in 2016, November 14 in 2017, November 20 in 2018, November 19 in 2019, November 5 in 2020, October 21 in 2021, October 31 in 2022, November 15 in 2023, November 11 in 2024, and November 12 in 2025. In every one of those years but one the road outlasted the cables by at least 19 days, and in seven of the ten seasons the park lists both dates the gap was three weeks or more. The exception was 2021, when the road shut nine days after the cables came down. The window is real and it is not guaranteed, which is exactly the kind of plan this site exists to make honestly.

Photo: Cory Goehring

What an October day up here feels like comes from the Park Service's climate table for Tuolumne Meadows, at 8,600 feet: an average high of 57 and an average low of 26 in October, against 65 and 32 in September, with October's precipitation more than three times September's. By November the averages are 47 and 20. So the trailhead is frosted at dawn, the creek crossing is trivial, the summit is cold and can be windy, the thunderstorm season is largely behind you, and the first real storm of the winter is a possibility on any given week. Layers, a warm hat, and a hard look at the forecast the night before replace the lightning rule as the day's discipline. The days are shorter too; the eight-to-ten-hour estimate does not shrink because the light did.

Three dates govern the autumn version and they arrive within a day of each other. Overnight parking on Tioga Road, including the Sunrise Lakes trailhead, ends October 15, which closes the overnight option below for the rest of the season. The Tuolumne Meadows Wilderness Center closes around October 14, after which wilderness permits are by self-registration at the station. And the cables, as above, come down in the middle of that same week. From mid-October to the road closure, then, Clouds Rest is a day hike, from a car you drive up that morning, in conditions you check the night before on the conditions page and the bulletin. Put another way: it is the highest walk-up summit in the park, with no permit, no bus, and almost nobody on it. The fall piece lays out the rest of what the season closes and opens, and the dates page keeps the cables rule and the road on one calendar.

Staying out: the overnight version

Clouds Rest as a backpack is an easier day and a better summit, because you can be on the ridge at dawn or in the last light without a 14-mile day around it. Two rules from the permit system shape it.

A wilderness permit is required for any night out, and the Park Service is explicit that none is needed for a day hike, with one exception, Half Dome. The Sunrise Lakes trailhead carries a daily quota of nine lottery permits and six first-come, first-served, and it is one of the trailheads marked Half Dome eligible, meaning a backpacker starting here can apply for the Half Dome add-on, which is required even for permit holders whenever the cables are up. Sixty percent of the quota goes through the weekly lottery 24 weeks ahead and the remaining forty percent is released seven days ahead; reservations carry a $10 fee per permit and $5 per person. The wilderness permit guide walks the whole system, and the first-backpack piece covers what the permit does not tell you.

Once you are out, the wilderness regulations apply as written: an approved bear-resistant canister is required for food storage in all areas of the Yosemite Wilderness, camps go at least 100 feet from any water and any trail and at least a mile from any road, campfires are prohibited above 9,600 feet (which rules out the summit ridge and the upper mountain), and groups are capped at 15. The forested basin between the two climbs is where most parties sleep on the way up, high enough to make the summit morning short and below the treeline for the night; pick a site that meets the distance rules rather than the first flat spot by the pond.

And the seasonal limit again, because it is the one that catches people: no overnight parking on Tioga Road from October 15. The backpack is a summer and early-autumn plan. After the middle of October, the only Clouds Rest is the day hike.

From the Valley, and the through-hike

There is a longer Clouds Rest, and it is the one the strong parties post about. The mountain can be approached from below, up the Half Dome corridor from Happy Isles through Little Yosemite Valley and then on to the summit, and the Park Service's own guided version, run with the Yosemite Conservancy, is a three-day, 17-mile through-hike from Tenaya Lake to Yosemite Valley over the top. Do the arithmetic against the seven-mile approach from Tenaya and the Valley side comes out at roughly ten miles one way, which puts a Valley round trip at about twenty miles with all of Half Dome's climbing and then some. A fit party with a very early start does it as a day; a few add the Half Dome summit on the way back, with a permit, and turn it into an all-day epic. Neither is the Clouds Rest this article recommends to someone deciding what to do with a Tuesday.

The through-hike is the better plan for the ambitious. Two cars, or the YARTS run while it operates, and a wilderness permit from the Sunrise Lakes quota; the climb is done from the high side, the summit comes on day one or day two, and the descent drops through Little Yosemite Valley to the Valley floor. Little Yosemite Valley has no potable water and a campground with communal food lockers; the Merced is the water source and it must be treated. Note that from the Happy Isles side the rules run the other way, with no camping between the Valley and Little Yosemite Valley and a first night required at the campground there.

Half Dome or Clouds Rest, when you can have either

If you hold a Half Dome permit and the day is dry, hike Half Dome. The cables are singular and there is nothing else like them in any national park; the lottery guide covers the two lotteries, including the daily one that runs two days ahead of the hike, from midnight to 4 pm Pacific, for a share of the 300 places a day. The Park Service caps that trail at about 225 day hikers and 75 backpackers.

If you do not hold one, or the date is outside the cable season, or the idea of the Mist Trail in August makes the decision for you, Clouds Rest is not the second choice. It is a higher summit, a bigger view, a cooler start, a trail with a fraction of the company, and a ridge walk that asks something of you without asking for a harness. The people who have done both split more evenly than you would expect. Go stand on the spine and decide for yourself.

The practical close, then: check conditions the night before, drive to the Sunrise Lakes trailhead before first light, carry three liters and a warm layer, be on top before noon, stay off the ridge if it is wet or the sky is building, and turn around without argument if either happens. Fourteen miles, 2,300 feet, no permit, and Half Dome under your boots.
