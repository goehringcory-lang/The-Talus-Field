January in Yosemite starts as the end of one trip and turns, within two days, into another. The first two days of the month are the tail of the holiday week, when the Park Service warns of traffic and limited parking and the hotels sell at full price. From January 3 the crowd goes home, the winter discounts come back, and the park settles into the longest quiet stretch it has all year, five or six weeks of short days, wet storms, and a Valley floor with room on it, until the February firefall brings the crowds back. That second January is the one this guide is about.

What follows is the month as the park's own records describe it: the weather normals, the road and trail history, the ski area's season, the campground calendar, and the rates the concessioner publishes. The season-wide case, including the full chain-control explainer, Dewey Point, and the ranger-led snowshoe walks, is in the winter guide. This piece is about what is specific to January.

The weather, in numbers

The Park Service's 1991 to 2020 normals for Yosemite Valley, at about 4,000 feet, give January an average high of 48°F and an average low of 29°F, with 7.0 inches of precipitation. That last figure makes January the wettest month of the year in the Valley, ahead of February at 6.5 inches and December at 5.6. The average high is two degrees warmer than December's, so January is not the coldest month by day, but its nights sit just below freezing on average, and the park says it receives more than 75 percent of its precipitation between November and March.

Whether that water arrives as rain or snow on the Valley floor is decided storm by storm. The park's own phrasing is that the Valley "can be rainy or snowy in any given winter storm," and that is the honest forecast for any January date booked more than a week ahead. Higher up there is no question. Tuolumne Meadows, at 8,600 feet, averages a January high of 41°F and a low of 13°F, with 6.2 inches of precipitation, and the park describes most of Yosemite as blanketed in snow from about November through May. Pack for a wet day and a cold clear one, and plan on frost every night.

What is open, and what the road record says

Yosemite Valley and Wawona are reachable by car all year, the park says, and they are the whole of a January trip by road. Everything above them has been decided since November.

Tioga Road is closed. Once it closes for the season, the park allows no vehicles between Crane Flat and Tioga Pass, Tuolumne Meadows included. The notes to the park's historical dataset record only two winters in which the road reopened after closing, and both openings reached into January: from December 23, 1999, to January 1, 2000, and from December 16, 2011, to January 17, 2012. Neither is a plan. Olmsted Point, Tenaya Lake, and the meadows are off a January itinerary.

Glacier Point Road is plowed only to Badger Pass. From mid-December through early April, the park clears the first stretch of the road as far as the Badger Pass Ski Area and no farther. The drive-up view from Glacier Point is not part of any January trip; the rim is reachable only on skis or snowshoes, which is the next section's business.

The Mariposa Grove is open, on foot. The grove road is closed in winter and the shuttle from the Welcome Plaza near the South Entrance runs no later than November 30. The park describes the Washburn Trail as a two-mile route to the grove with 500 feet of elevation gain, and warns that it may be snowy or icy enough that skis or snowshoes are the right tools. The sequoias in snow are one of the best things the month offers, and the full walk-in plan is in the Mariposa Grove guide.

The trails: which ones January closes

The park says the only trails it routinely closes are three: the Mist Trail to Vernal Fall, the stretch of the John Muir Trail between Clark Point and the top of Nevada Fall, and the Four Mile Trail to Glacier Point. All three are usually gated by January. The park's record of seasonal trail dates shows the Four Mile Trail open for at least part of only four of the fifteen Januaries from 2011 through 2025: 2012, 2015, 2018, and 2024. In two of those, the 2017 and 2023 seasons, both the Four Mile and the Mist Trail stayed open into the new year, closing on January 18, 2018, and January 7, 2024. A January visitor should plan as though both are closed and treat an open gate as luck.

What stays open is the Valley floor, and a January Valley floor is a good place to walk. The Yosemite Falls Trail is the month's most reliable climb, for a reason the park spells out: its lower portion takes direct sun in winter, so it can be snow-free while other trails are icy, and the park calls Columbia Rock, a mile up, worthwhile at any time of year. Above Columbia Rock the trail gets little or no winter sun and can be very icy or buried, and the park warns that early mornings and late afternoons, when the ice is hardest, are the most dangerous hours on it. The route, the turnaround points, and the ice cone at the base of the upper fall are in the Yosemite Falls Trail guide; Mirror Lake, the Lower Yosemite Fall loop, and the Valley Loop Trail are in the winter hiking guide.

Two habits keep a January walk from ending badly. Carry traction cleats for the paved paths, which glaze in the shade and stay glazed, and plan the longest walk for the middle of the day, when the sun reaches the floor.

Badger Pass in January

If the winter operations are the point of the trip, January sits wholly inside their published season. The concessioner describes the Badger Pass downhill area as operating daily from 9 a.m. to 4 p.m., mid-December to mid-March, conditions permitting. It is a small hill by design: 7,200 to 8,000 feet, an 800-foot vertical drop, ten runs, and five lifts, with 35 percent of the terrain rated beginner. Full-day lift tickets can be bought online; half-day and lower-lift tickets are sold at the lodge.

The Nordic center at Badger is the trailhead for the winter high country. The concessioner lists 90 miles of marked trails and 25 miles of groomed track, including the 21-mile round trip on the snow-covered road to Glacier Point, with rentals, lessons, and guided tours from the center, which is open daily from 8:30 a.m. to 4 p.m. in season. Its opening date depends on snow: the concessioner's page, as of this writing, still carries last season's notice that the Nordic center opened on Friday, January 9. A December plan built around cross-country skiing can fail for want of snow; a January one is on firmer ground, though not certain.

The tubing hill is the most January-specific thing at Badger. The concessioner gives its anticipated operating dates as January through March, weather permitting, because the hill is built entirely from natural snow and no snowmaking can open it early. Sessions run from 11 a.m. to 1 p.m. and from 1:30 to 3:30 p.m., at $30 per person for two hours, for riders 42 inches and taller, and there is no conveyor: you carry the tube up. The park's free shuttle runs between the Valley and Badger Pass whenever the ski area's facilities are open, which the park says is typically mid-December through March, so a January visitor who does not want to chain up on Badger Pass Road can leave the car in the Valley.

Down on the Valley floor, the Curry Village ice rink needs neither snow nor skill. The concessioner says families have skated there since 1928, and it sells tickets online for the full season and at the Curry Village tour and activity desk. As of this writing, its opening date is still to be determined, so confirm the rink is open before you promise a skate to anyone.

The holiday tail, then the quiet

The Park Service puts the season's crowd plainly on its winter page: Yosemite "may be very busy during winter holidays, especially in late December and early January, with traffic congestion and limited parking possible." The concessioner draws the same line in its rate calendar. Every one of its current winter offers, at the Ahwahnee, Yosemite Valley Lodge, and Curry Village, carries the same blackout window, December 23 through January 2, and a second one, February 12 through 20, which covers the firefall's peak and Presidents' Day weekend.

Read the other way, those blackouts mark the longest unbroken run of winter discounts: between January 3 and February 11, all three offers are in force. On the concessioner's specials page, the Ahwahnee runs from $389 on select two-night stays (suites excluded) and Yosemite Valley Lodge from $172 on stays of two nights or more; the Curry Village offer is a discount on two-night stays on select dates from December 13 through March 25. Rates move with the date, so treat those figures as the low end, not a promise. The concessioner takes the first night as a deposit at booking and refunds it on cancellations made more than seven days out.

There is also no entrance reservation to plan around: the park dropped its timed-entry system for 2026, so entry needed only the standard pass, $35 for a private vehicle and valid for seven days. Check the park's reservations page for the year you travel. The 2026 calendar of free entrance days had no date in January (the first was Presidents' Day, February 16), and visitors who are not US residents should read the international visitors page first, because the park's fee page adds a $100 per-person fee for non-residents 16 and older.

Driving in: chains, every day

The park's guidance is to expect chain requirements from November through March, "even if you only plan to visit Yosemite Valley and use the free shuttle." The rule that catches visitors is about carrying, not mounting: whenever a chain control is in effect, every vehicle must have chains in it, four-wheel drives and rentals included. Rental companies generally do not supply them, the park notes, and a rental with all-season tires is not exempt. Chains or cables matching your tire size can be bought at auto parts stores and gas stations in the gateway towns, and in limited supply at the Village Garage in the Valley and the Wawona gas station; the park says businesses that rent them are hard to find. Plastic straps and other emergency devices do not meet the requirement.

The three control levels decide whether the chains go on. Under R1, a car, pickup, or SUV under 6,000 pounds with snow tires on at least two drive wheels may skip them; under R2, a vehicle under 6,500 pounds with four-wheel or all-wheel drive engaged and snow tires on all four wheels may skip them; under R3, everyone chains up. The park adds that many battery-electric trucks and some large electric SUVs exceed those weights and must chain up even when they have the right tires. The speed limit in a chain control area is 25 mph, and failing to put chains on when required carries a citation of up to $5,000.

Route choice matters more in January than at any other time. The park says the Wawona Road (Highway 41), the Big Oak Flat Road (Highway 120 from the west), and Badger Pass Road need chains more often than the El Portal Road (Highway 140), the Valley's own roads, and the Hetch Hetchy Road. Highway 140 is also the one route YARTS runs all year, from Merced through Mariposa, Midpines, and El Portal, and the park suggests parking at a YARTS stop on 140 below the chain control and riding in as the alternative to chaining up at all. The road-conditions recording, 209/372-0200, then 1, then 1, is updated whenever conditions change and is the call to make the morning you drive; the entrance-by-entrance comparison is in the entrances guide.

Where to sleep in January

In the park, the Ahwahnee, Yosemite Valley Lodge, and Curry Village carry the month. Curry Village sells two kinds of canvas tent cabin, and the difference matters in January: the heated tents have a heater and one outlet, and the concessioner runs the heat from the Tuesday after Labor Day to the Friday before Memorial Day, while the unheated tents are exactly that, with extra blankets at the front desk. Neither kind has plumbing, so the bathroom is a walk on what may be an icy path. Curry's wood cabins with bath and the Stoneman rooms are heated. The Wawona Hotel is closed: the concessioner says the Park Service is conducting a condition assessment of the building and that there is no anticipated reopening date.

Camping in January is a short list. Upper Pines is the only campground on reservations all year, and its January dates sell on the park's monthly calendar, five months out at 7 a.m. Pacific: arrivals from December 15 through January 14 open on August 15, and arrivals from January 15 through February 14 open on September 15. By the time most people plan a January trip, both windows have sold, and the realistic route in is a cancellation. Camp 4, Wawona, and Hodgdon Meadow are first come, first served in winter, though the park notes they can fill on holidays and weekends. Sleeping in a vehicle is not allowed anywhere in the park outside a campsite you have registered for. Plan for nights below freezing and a tent that can carry snow.

Outside the park, the towns on Highway 140 put you on the lowest road and the year-round bus line, which is the strongest January argument for Mariposa or El Portal. The trade-offs among the in-park properties are laid out in the lodging guide, and the town-by-town comparison is on the lodging board.

A naturalist's January

The waterfalls come back in January if the month is doing its job. The park's description of the winter is that water levels tend to be low, but that once some rain and snow have fallen, Yosemite Falls begins flowing again, though not at springtime levels. In the wettest month of the year, that usually means a waterfall with some voice to it, and in a cold spell the spray from the upper fall builds the ice cone at its base that the falls guide describes.

The bears are less asleep than visitors assume. The park says Yosemite's black bears hibernate in hollow trees and logs, under root masses, and in rock jumbles, but that their sleep "is not a deep one" and that they often leave the den periodically. The park's naturalists noticed the same thing almost a century ago. The January 1929 Yosemite Nature Notes reported that although winter snows had come a full month early that season, at least a few Valley bears had skipped hibernation altogether, and that the sound of them playing with ash-can lids still kept light sleepers awake; the bulletin's best explanation was an abundant food supply. The lesson travels well. The park's food-storage rules carry a fine of up to $5,000 and are written without a season, so a January visitor stores food exactly as a July one does: in the locker, latched.

The rest of the month's natural history is about light and water. The Valley's walls take the sun off the floor early, and the clearing hour after a storm, when cloud tears off the walls and every ledge on El Capitan carries snow, is the photograph the season is known for. That is the reason to keep one day of a January trip unscheduled.

How to build a January trip

Come after January 2. The first two days of the month belong to the holiday crowd and the full-price calendar. From January 3 until the firefall blackout begins on February 12, the park is at its quietest and the in-park rooms at their winter rates, so a midweek stay in that window is the version of January most worth having. If you want Horsetail Fall as well, that is a February trip with its own rules, set out each season on the firefall page.

Then pick the trip by the snow. If skiing, tubing, or snowshoeing is the point, the month's odds are better than December's, but the tubing hill and the Nordic trails run on natural snow, so confirm Badger is open before you drive up. If the point is the Valley, any week works: walk the Yosemite Falls Trail to Columbia Rock, the Lower Fall loop, and Mirror Lake, and walk into the Mariposa Grove on a clear day.

Read the conditions the morning of. Chain controls, road closures, trail gates, and the ski area change by the day in January. The conditions page collects the park's live feeds and phone numbers in one place. Come up 140 with chains in the trunk, sleep in or near the Valley, keep one day free for the storm, and spend the clear day after it outside.

January rooms, after the holiday week

From January 3 until the firefall weeks in mid-February, the concessioner's winter rates are in force at the Ahwahnee, Yosemite Valley Lodge, and Curry Village, and the gateway towns on Highway 140 are at their quietest. A search on your dates shows quickly how much room the month has.
