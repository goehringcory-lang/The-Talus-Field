Snowshoeing in Yosemite happens in three places, and none of them is the Valley floor. The park marks winter routes at Badger Pass on Glacier Point Road, at Crane Flat on the Big Oak Flat Road, and in the Mariposa Grove, and those three, at 7,200, 6,200, and about 5,100 feet, are where the snow lasts long enough to walk on. Down at 4,000 feet the Valley's snow melts off the flat ground within days of a storm, and what it leaves behind is ice, which is a job for traction cleats, not snowshoes. So the first decision is not which trail. It is which of the three, and that depends on how much snow has fallen, how far you want to walk, and whether you want company.

This is the guide to all three: the routes the Park Service names, the ranger-led and guided walks, the rentals, and the rules that keep a snowshoe day from turning into a search. The rest of the season, Badger's lifts and the chain rules included, is in the winter guide.

When there is snow to walk on

The park's estimate is that its marked winter routes generally have enough snow from mid to late December through mid-March, and it adds that this varies from year to year. The last two winters show how much. The Badger Pass Nordic center, which rents the snowshoes and grooms the trails, did not open at all in the 2024 to 2025 season and opened on January 9 in 2025 to 2026; the lifts closed for the season on March 18. A snowshoe trip planned for Christmas week is a bet; one planned for late January or February is a better one.

The routes are marked, but not the way a summer trail is. The park says winter routes carry metal signs and tree markers, colored triangles or rectangles, that can fall or be buried by new snow, and that the tracks already in the snow may not lead where you want to go. Few trails in the park are marked for winter use at all. On a clear day after a week of traffic the way is obvious. In a storm, or the morning after one, it is not.

Badger Pass and the Glacier Point Road

Badger Pass has the park's largest marked network, and it is the place to start. The ski area sits five miles up Glacier Point Road from the Wawona Road, at a base of 7,200 feet; beyond it the park closes the road to cars and grooms it, in part, for skiing and snowshoeing. Restrooms are at the ski area, and there is a pit toilet at Summit Meadow, about a mile out. One rule of the road: snowshoers stay outside the parallel ski tracks, and the center lane belongs to skate skiers and beginners. Walking in the classic track ruins it for everyone behind you.

The destination most people have heard of is Dewey Point, a granite overlook on the Valley rim with El Capitan across the gap. The park lists two routes to it, the Meadow route and the Ridge route, both ungroomed, both starting from the Badger Pass area. The round trip runs seven to eight miles depending on the route; the concessioner describes its guided version as about eight miles and a full day. Budget the whole day, start by mid-morning, and turn around by the hour you set, not the hour you reach the rim. The view from Dewey Point in summer, and the road it hangs off, are in the Glacier Point guide.

Shorter days are easy to build from the same lot. The groomed road and the semi-loop to Old Badger summit are the gentlest walking in the network, and Summit Meadow, a mile out with a toilet waiting, makes a turnaround a first-timer can trust.

Crane Flat: the quiet one

Crane Flat sits on the Big Oak Flat Road (Highway 120 from the west), at about 6,200 feet, near where Tioga Road closes for the winter. The park's routes there start near the Tuolumne Grove and Merced Grove parking areas, and they are not groomed. Two are named:

  • Crane Flat Lookout, 1.5 miles one way, out and back.
  • The Gin Flat Loop, 6.25 miles round trip.

Vault toilets are at both grove trailheads. The park lists no rentals here, so bring your own snowshoes; what you get in exchange is a trailhead without a ski area's traffic. Crane Flat is also where the park's snow play area is, near the cross-country routes and open in winter when conditions allow, so expect sleds by the road and quiet once you are out on the routes. The Big Oak Flat Road is one of the roads the park says needs chains more often than the rest, so carry them. One rule specific to the area: the park does not allow camping in the Tuolumne Grove. The groves themselves, and why the sequoias grow where they do, are the subject of the sequoia guide.

The Mariposa Grove: sequoias in snow

The grove road closes to cars in winter, and the grove stays open to anyone on foot. The park's winter route is the Washburn Trail, a two-mile route from the Welcome Plaza near the South Entrance to the grove's Arrival Area, with 500 feet of elevation gain; skiers and snowshoers can also use the closed road. The park says the trail suits snowshoers better than inexperienced skiers, and that at about 5,100 feet the snow here is less reliable than at Badger or Crane Flat. There is a restroom at the parking area and a vault toilet partway up the trail.

That lower elevation cuts both ways. In a lean month the trail may be bare or icy, and boots with cleats will serve better than snowshoes. After a fresh storm, it is one of the best snowshoe walks in the park, and the reward for the climb is the Grizzly Giant under snow with almost no one around it. The full grove plan, including the walk to the Grizzly Giant, is in the Mariposa Grove guide.

The ranger walks, and the guided ones

Ranger-led snowshoe walks are free. The Park Service says rangers may lead them from Badger Pass from mid-December through March, conditions and staffing permitting, and lists them on the park calendar when they run. It does not publish a season schedule in advance, so look at the calendar for the days of your trip, and read "conditions and staffing permitting" as a real condition. In a thin winter, check the calendar the day before you drive up.

The park also names two organizations that lead guided snowshoe walks, Yosemite Conservancy and the Yosemite Mountaineering School. The Conservancy had not posted a 2026 to 2027 winter program as of this writing. The Mountaineering School's guides run two walks from the Badger Pass Nordic center, at the prices the concessioner currently posts:

  • Discovery snowshoe hike: three hours and about three to four miles, Mondays, Tuesdays, and Saturdays at 9 a.m. and 1 p.m., $85 including rentals, ages 10 and up with an adult.
  • Dewey Point snowshoe hike: all day and about eight miles round trip, Sundays, Wednesdays, and Fridays at 9 a.m., $125 including rentals, a group limit of seven, ages 14 and up with an adult.

One logistics note from the concessioner: the free Badger Pass shuttle from the Valley arrives at about 9:30 a.m., and the guides will not hold a 9 a.m. hike for it. Families with children under 10 can arrange a private hike through the Nordic center at 209/372-4996 or the Mountaineering School at 209/372-8344.

Rentals and what to carry

Snowshoes rent at the Badger Pass Nordic center, first come, first served, open daily from 8:30 a.m. to 4 p.m. in season. The concessioner's two pages disagree on the all-day price, $26.50 on its snowshoeing page and $30 on its cross-country page; both list $24 for a half day. Rentals are due back by 4 p.m. or you may be charged another day. If your plan is Crane Flat or the Mariposa Grove, rent at Badger the day before or bring your own; the park lists no rentals at either.

The park's winter safety page sets the rest of the kit. Dress in wool or synthetic layers, not cotton, and adjust them before you overheat or chill. Carry a map and a compass and know how to use them, even if you have a GPS, because the routes are under snow and so are their markers. Bring water and food for longer than you plan to be out; the days are short, and the cold makes everything slower. Poles help on every route. Traction cleats belong in the pack too, for the icy stretch between the car and the snow.

Safety: the four things that go wrong

Getting lost. The park's own words are that trails and markers are covered with snow and that finding the way requires advanced navigation skills. Tell someone responsible where you are going and when you will be back, and turn around if the tracks you are following stop matching the map.

Avalanches. Some areas of the park are prone to them. The park's rule for anyone without avalanche training is plain: do not go on or beneath slopes steeper than 20 degrees. Dewey Point is also the edge of a cliff, and snow hides where the rock ends. Stay well back from the lip.

Cold and wet. The park warns that weather can change rapidly and dramatically, that winter conditions generally run from November through April, though early and late storms can come at any time, and that warm, wet storms add the risk of hypothermia. It asks you to watch for hypothermia, exhaustion, and dehydration in your companions as well as yourself.

The short day. Winter light quits early, and a snowshoe mile is slower than a summer one. Start by mid-morning, set a turnaround time, and keep it.

Staying out overnight

An overnight trip on snowshoes needs a free wilderness permit, picked up in person at the station closest to your starting point; the Badger Pass Ranger Station covers the Glacier Point Road routes, and the Big Oak Flat Information Station and Wawona Visitor Center are among the others. Reservations are not available from November through April. The park caps winter departures by trailhead: 25 a day on the Dewey Point Meadow route and 10 on the Ridge route, for example, with 10 at Crane Flat and 8 at the Mariposa Grove.

The winter camping rules are specific. Camp at least a mile from any plowed road and a mile and a half from the Badger Pass Ski Area boundary, out of sight of trails and water. Camping is not allowed at Summit Meadow, or on Dewey Point or Glacier Point themselves, though it is allowed near the two points, and it is not allowed in the Tuolumne Grove. Cook on a stove; the park allows open fires for emergencies only. Pets are not allowed. For a roof instead of a tent, the Ostrander Ski Hut, run by Yosemite Conservancy about 10 miles from Badger Pass, is open from late December to early April, booked by a fall lottery and then first come, first served, and the Conservancy describes the route in as for experienced skiers only. As of mid-October 2026 the trails south of Glacier Point Road toward Ostrander Lake were still closed for the Dome Fire; the Dewey Point routes, north of the road, were not in the closure. Check the park's closures before you plan either.

Reading the snow, the old way

The park's naturalists have been taking people out on snowshoes for nearly a century, and they always came back talking about tracks. In the March 1933 Yosemite Nature Notes, the junior park naturalist C. C. Presnall described a snowshoe trip he made with the assistant park naturalist M. E. Beatty to Big Meadows, under six miles round trip and, he wrote, one of the easiest winter trips out of the Valley. Deer tracks were the most numerous, concentrated on the warm north side of the canyon where the chaparral offered browse. They found one coyote track, two of fox, and two of a wildcat, and the surprise of the day was a mink track two-tenths of a mile from the river. Around the old ranch buildings, where they had expected mouse tracks, there were none, and the dainty prints of a ring-tailed cat suggested why.

That is still the best reason to go slowly. A snowshoe walk is the one time of year the forest writes down who passed through it overnight, and the morning after a light snow is the clearest page you will get.

Which one, then

For a first time, go to Badger Pass, rent at the Nordic center, and either join a ranger walk if the calendar has one or walk the groomed road to Summit Meadow and back, staying outside the ski tracks. For the big day, snowshoe to Dewey Point, with a guide or with a map, a turnaround time, and the whole of a short winter day. For solitude, bring your own snowshoes to Crane Flat and walk out to the lookout. For the sequoias, wait for a fresh storm and climb the Washburn Trail into the Mariposa Grove. And if the snow is thin, leave the snowshoes in the car: the Valley floor's open trails, in the winter hiking guide, want cleats, not webbing. Check the conditions page, the Yosemite webcams for whether snow is lying, and the park calendar the morning you go.
