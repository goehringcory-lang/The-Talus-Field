The most-asked question I get in Yosemite Valley is not about waterfalls or bears. It is asked at about eleven in the morning, through a rolled-down window, by someone on their third slow lap of the same one-way road: where do I park? It is a fair question and it has a real answer, but the answer is not a secret lot. It is a different plan for the day, and the earlier you make it the cheaper it is. This is the whole picture: which lots exist, what they are actually for, when they fill, and what to do when you have already lost that race.

The three lots that matter

Yosemite Valley is seven miles long and it has three real day-use parking areas. Everything else is a trailhead pullout, a picnic turnout, or a lot reserved for people who are sleeping there. The Park Service's own advice names the three and then tells you what to do next: park at Yosemite Village, Yosemite Falls, or Curry Village, and see the Valley by shuttle, bike path, and trail. That is the entire official strategy, and it is correct.

Yosemite Village parking. The big one, and the default. It sits in the middle of the Valley next to the Welcome Center, the store, the food, and the museum, and it is the hub the free shuttle radiates from. If you have no particular plan, this is the lot to aim at, because everything else in the Valley is reachable from it without moving the car again.

Yosemite Falls parking. On Northside Drive by Yosemite Valley Lodge, and the closest legal parking to the Lower Yosemite Fall trail, which is the single most-walked path in the park. It is a shuttle stop, and it has a bank of Level 2 electric-vehicle chargers, which is worth knowing because there is not much charging in the Valley and what exists gets occupied early.

Curry Village parking. The east-Valley lot, closest to the Mist Trail corridor, Mirror Lake, and the Pines campgrounds. Some Park Service pages and a certain amount of surviving signage still call this Half Dome Village, a name the concessioner used for a few years in the middle of the last decade; it is the same place. Two practical notes: there are no restrooms in the lot itself, and sleeping in a vehicle or camper there is not allowed, which people discover at two in the morning more often than you would think.

A thing the Park Service does not publish, and which I will therefore not invent: space counts. You will find confident numbers for each of these lots on the wider internet. None of them come from NPS, they disagree with each other, and the capacity that matters on any given morning is not the striped total anyway. It is how many people got there before you.

The trailheads, and why most of them have no parking

The single most common parking mistake in the Valley is driving to a trailhead. Most of the famous ones have no lot at all. Happy Isles, where the Mist Trail begins, is a shuttle stop, not a parking area; there is no drive-up spot at the footbridge and there never was. Mirror Lake is the same. The Valley's trailhead parking is a small, specific inventory documented on the Park Service's trailhead parking page, and a good part of it exists to serve wilderness permit holders leaving a car for several days rather than day hikers arriving at ten.

So the sequence for a Valley hike is: park in one of the three day lots, ride to the trailhead, walk. Not: find the trailhead, then find parking. The Mist Trail piece assumes this and so does every ranger you will ask.

When they fill

The Park Service's language is careful and worth reading literally: during the busy summer season, especially on weekends, parking fills early and heavy traffic is common in Yosemite Valley. It does not name an hour, because the hour moves. What it does name is a window. Most vehicle congestion happens between ten in the morning and six in the evening, and the official recommendation is to be inside the park before nine, or to wait and come in after five.

Treat that as the outer bound rather than the target. The advice on this site is an hour stricter in both directions, and it is stricter for a reason: on a busy day the lots fill before the congestion window opens, so nine o'clock is already a gamble. Be through the gate by eight, and before seven on a summer weekend. Coming in the other way, four in the afternoon works as well as five. The crowd forecast makes the same case from a decade of visitation data.

In practice, from twenty seasons of watching it: on a July or August Saturday the Valley lots are effectively gone by mid-morning and the ones nearest the trailheads go first. On a Tuesday in October you can arrive at noon and park at Curry Village without drama. Between those two extremes is every other day of the year, and the variable that predicts it best is not the season, it is the day of the week. The Park Service says this too, in its own dry way, by recommending Monday through Thursday.

You do not have to guess on the day. Text YNPTRAFFIC to 333111 to get park traffic alerts on your phone, and check the conditions page before you leave your room, which is where the live entrance-station waits live. Spring through fall, the park's own typical figures run to an hour or two at the South Entrance on Highway 41 and about half an hour at Arch Rock, Big Oak Flat, and Tioga Pass. Those are ordinary days, not the worst ones; a summer holiday weekend can put a queue miles back from the gate. And all of it is time spent before you have parked anything.

What to do at eleven o'clock

Say the plan already failed. You are in the Valley, it is late morning, the lots are full, and you are on your second lap. Here is the order I would work through, best option first.

  1. Stop circling and take the next legal space you see, even the wrong one. A space at Curry Village when you wanted Yosemite Falls costs you one shuttle ride. A third lap costs you most of an hour and may end in the same place. The lots do turn over; midday is when the dawn hikers come out.
  2. Leave and come back at five. This sounds like a defeat and it is the best move on the list. Drive out to a gateway town, eat lunch somewhere with a table, and return in the late afternoon when the day-trippers are leaving and the light is doing the thing you came for. The Valley between five and sunset is a substantially better park than the Valley at noon.
  3. Go somewhere that is not the Valley. This is now official park guidance and not just local advice: the 2026 congestion strategy explicitly includes promoting recreation outside Yosemite Valley. If Tioga Road is open, the high country has parking when the Valley has none. So does Wawona, so does Hetch Hetchy, and the Mariposa Grove has its own lot at the Welcome Plaza with its own shuttle.
  4. Park once, then stay out of the car. Whatever space you end up in, treat it as final. The Valley has more than twelve miles of paved bike path, a seasonal bike share that Yosemite Conservancy makes free for short trips, and a shuttle system that reaches everything. The shuttle and YARTS guide covers how that actually works.
  5. Next time, do not bring the car in at all. YARTS runs into the Valley on Highway 140 year-round and seasonally from Fresno, Sonora, and Mammoth. A bus rider has no parking problem to solve, which is a strange and pleasant way to spend a day in a park where everyone else is solving one.
The reason there is no secret lot is that the Valley is a seven-mile room with granite on both sides. There is no more floor to give.

Where you may not park

Park in a designated space, or in a paved turnout with the vehicle completely off the road. That is the whole rule, and the two ways people break it are both common. Driving or parking on vegetation is prohibited, which includes the meadow edges that look like they have been driven on before, because they have, and that is the damage. Blocking a travel lane on a road with no shoulder is the other one, and on the Valley loop it is worse than rude: the road is one lane in each direction for much of its length and an emergency vehicle has nowhere to go around you. Rangers cite and tow. In midsummer they do it constantly.

Accessible parking

Designated accessible spaces exist at each of the main lots and at several of the picnic areas. Two things are worth knowing beyond that. A disability placard is not just a parking credential in Yosemite: it also permits driving on a couple of roads otherwise closed to private vehicles, specifically the Happy Isles Loop Road and Mirror Lake Road, which converts two of the Valley's shuttle-dependent destinations into drive-up ones. And if you arrive without your placard, or your need is temporary, entrance stations and visitor centers issue temporary ones. Our accessibility guide goes through the rest of it, including who to call before you come.

RVs, trailers, and the vehicles that do not fit

Length limits are enforced by geometry, not by policy, which is why they are absolute. Vehicles over 45 feet are not permitted on the roads into Yosemite Valley at all. Highway 140 through the Merced canyon is the forgiving approach, with no tunnels and roughly 60 feet of combined length allowed; the tunnels on Highways 41 and 120 drop to about 10 feet 2 inches of clearance at the curb, which is where the roof damage happens. Beyond the Sentinel Dome and Taft Point trailhead, Glacier Point Road caps single vehicles at 30 feet and bans trailers outright.

In the Valley itself, the practical answer for a large rig is Curry Village, which has RV parking near its eastbound shuttle stop. The better answer is to leave the rig at the campground and take the shuttle, because a 32-foot motorhome looking for a space in a full lot at noon is the single least happy vehicle in Yosemite.

One more thing about the loop

The Valley road system is a loop and much of it is one-way. Southside Drive runs eastbound, into the Valley; Northside Drive runs westbound, out of it. This is why the "just circle back" instinct is so expensive here: you cannot turn around and retry a lot you just passed. You commit to the loop and get another look in twenty minutes. Knowing that in advance changes how you drive it. Pick your lot before you enter the Valley, not as you pass it.

2026: no reservation, and what replaced it

There is no day-use or peak-hours vehicle reservation for Yosemite in 2026. The systems that ran in most years between 2020 and 2025 are gone, including the February weekend reservations for Horsetail Fall. The park's stated reasoning is that its own 2025 analysis found most weekdays held available parking, stable traffic flow, and visitation inside operational capacity, so a season-wide requirement was not the most effective tool.

What replaced it is a set of operational measures rather than a gate: real-time traffic monitoring, active parking management in the Valley, more staff at key intersections during peak periods, road-condition alerts, and an explicit push toward weekday visits and toward parts of the park that are not Yosemite Valley. The practical translation for a visitor is simple and slightly uncomfortable. Nothing is now rationing the number of cars that arrive at the Valley on a Saturday in July except the number of spaces, and the spaces run out. The reservation system used to make your parking decision for you. Now you make it yourself, at seven in the morning or at five in the afternoon.

The short version

  1. Three day lots: Yosemite Village, Yosemite Falls, Curry Village. Aim at Yosemite Village if you have no plan.
  2. Be through the gate by eight, and before seven on a summer weekend. The official line is nine; the lots do not wait that long.
  3. Monday through Thursday is a different park from Saturday.
  4. Do not drive to a trailhead. Park once, then shuttle, bike, or walk.
  5. Southside Drive is one-way in, Northside is one-way out. Choose the lot before you commit.
  6. If it is eleven and everything is full, leave and come back at five. It is the best move, not the consolation prize.

The Valley holds what it holds. Every visitor who accepts that at the entrance station has a better day than the one still looking for a space at noon, and the difference between them is not luck. It is an hour.
