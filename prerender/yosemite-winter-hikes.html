The email arrives every year around the first week of December, from somebody with a booked room and a creeping suspicion: is there anything to actually hike in Yosemite in winter, or is it all snowshoes and postcards? The suspicion is half right. The park's trail inventory shrinks by something like two thirds when the snow settles in, the famous names on the closed list are exactly the ones people came for, and nothing at the entrance gate explains which is which. But the half that stays open is real hiking, on real trails, through the emptiest and best-looking version of this park, and most of it needs nothing more exotic than boots, traction cleats, and a respect for the clock. This is the inventory: what closes, what stays open underfoot, and the one honest climb of the season. The winter guide covers the rest of the season, chains, Badger Pass, the rink; this piece is only about the trails.

The season this article describes runs from the first lasting snow, usually late November, into April: the Valley floor melts out first after each storm, the shaded corners hold their ice longest, and the Four Mile Trail stays gated into May. Where you fall inside that window matters less than the day's actual conditions, which is the whole discipline of the thing.

How winter hiking works here: elevation, aspect, ice

Three facts organize everything below. First, elevation. The Valley floor sits at about 4,000 feet, low enough that snow falls, sticks for a while, and usually melts back off the flat ground within days; the rim above it sits at 7,000-plus and holds a snowpack all season. That two-thousand-foot difference is the entire difference between a trail on this list and a trail on the closed list.

Second, aspect. The Valley runs east to west, so its north side, the Yosemite Falls side, faces south and takes sun all day, while the south side and every shaded recess hold their snow and ice for weeks after a storm. A south-facing trail can be bare and dry three days after a foot of snow while a shaded stretch of the same valley is a luge run. This is why the season's one reliable climb is on the sunny wall, and why the Mist Trail's shaded stair is closed.

Third, ice. The thing that ends winter hikes in Yosemite is almost never an avalanche or a storm. It is a paved path that glazed overnight, or a shaded switchback polished by a hundred boots. Traction cleats, the strap-on spikes sold under a dozen names, cost little, weigh nothing, and convert most of the Valley's winter hazards back into walking; carry them and trekking poles on anything that climbs, and put the cleats on before the ice gets interesting rather than after. Add the short days, with the sun down before five in December and off the shaded floor well before that, and the rules of the season write themselves: start mid-morning after the night's ice softens, set a turnaround time, and be off the trail by the time the light leaves it. Check the Park Bulletin and the Park Service conditions page the morning you walk; every entry below is subject to the current storm.

Closed, so you can stop wondering

The famous absences first, because knowing them is what makes the rest of the list trustworthy.

  • The Four Mile Trail closes for the winter, typically from the first lasting snow into May. In lean stretches the Park Service sometimes opens the lower three miles to a gate below Union Point, and that lower section still carries ice in the shade; the switchbacks above stay shut. The loop it anchors is a May-through-fall project.
  • The Mist Trail's granite stair beside Vernal Fall closes in winter, along with the John Muir Trail between Clark Point and the top of Nevada Fall. This is not bureaucratic caution: icicles the size of furniture form on the overhanging rock above both stretches and fall onto the trail, and the spray that makes the stair famous in June makes it a sheet of ice in January. A winter route survives, covered below.
  • Half Dome is out of season: the cables typically come down the day after Columbus Day and do not go back up until the Friday before Memorial Day, and in between the summit is a mountaineering objective, not a hike. The Half Dome piece explains the system.
  • Everything on Tioga Road and upper Glacier Point Road, Cathedral Lakes, Tuolumne, Sentinel Dome, Taft Point, is behind closed gates and under feet of snow. The rim can be reached in winter, but on snowshoes or skis from Badger Pass, which is a different sport and a different article.

The Valley floor, which stays yours

The floor is the winter park's main stage, and its flat trails stay open all season, snow or not. The difference from summer is not access. It is that you will be nearly alone on them.

The Lower Yosemite Fall loop, about a mile, paved and flat, changes character completely in winter. The fall itself is modest until the late-winter melt begins, but the amphitheater compensates with the season's own set pieces: on cold mornings the spray builds a frost apron over the talus, and after storms the whole recess hangs with ice that booms when it cuts loose. The paved loop is exactly where the black-ice warning applies; this is a cleats-in-pocket walk, not a sneakers walk.

Cook's Meadow and Sentinel Meadow, a mile of boardwalk, are the winter photograph you already know: Half Dome and Yosemite Falls over frosted grass, at their best in the hour after a clearing storm and in the fog the Merced breathes on cold mornings. The boardwalk glazes overnight; short steps, and cleats if it shines.

Mirror Lake, about two miles round trip on pavement to the lake and roughly five as the Tenaya Creek loop when conditions allow, is a better winter walk than a summer one, for a simple hydrological reason: winter is when the lake starts being a lake again. The seasonal pool that shrinks to a meadow by August refills with the first storms, and the reflections that named the place come back while the crowds are elsewhere. Tenaya Canyon keeps the sun late and loses it early; expect ice in both directions and give the loop away on icy days rather than forcing it.

The Valley Loop Trail is the sleeper of the season. The full circuit runs about 13 miles and the half loop about 7.2, but nobody owes it a lap: it is a chain of flat segments along the base of the walls, and in winter the western reaches toward Bridalveil and El Capitan are as close to having Yosemite Valley to yourself as daylight gets. Pick a stretch, walk it out and back, and add Bridalveil Fall, a half-mile round trip that in winter trades its spring violence for rime and quiet.

The one real climb: Columbia Rock, and sometimes the top

The season's honest answer to "one serious hike" lives on the sunny wall. The Yosemite Falls Trail from Camp 4 climbs south-facing switchbacks that melt out fast between storms, which makes it the most reliably hikeable ascent in the winter park. Columbia Rock, about two miles round trip and 1,000 feet up several dozen switchbacks, is the destination that fits most winter days: a railed perch with the Valley laid out below, Half Dome and Sentinel Rock across the way, earned before lunch. Another half mile, some of it downhill, reaches the full frontal view of Upper Yosemite Fall, which in cold snaps runs past a growing cone of frozen spray at its base, the season's most underrated sight.

The continuation to the top of the fall, about 7.2 miles round trip with 2,700 feet of gain, is a conditions call, not a default. The upper third holds snow and ice long after Columbia Rock is dry, the final traverses are shaded and exposed, and the overlook's granite is no place for glazed boot soles. In a lean January it goes with cleats, poles, and an early turnaround honestly held; in a deep one it does not go at all. If you make the top, you will likely share one of the park's great summit views with single digits of people. Decide at Columbia Rock, not at the trailhead, and let the mountain overrule the plan.

The Merced corridor, by its winter route

The trail most first-time winter visitors ask about is the Mist Trail, and the winter version is a different, gentler, still worthwhile animal. The Vernal Fall footbridge stays open all season: about 1.6 miles round trip from Happy Isles with 400 feet of gain on a paved grade whose shaded stretches ice hard, cleats again. The footbridge view of Vernal Fall over a low, clear, green winter Merced is quieter than the spring spectacle and better composed.

Above the bridge, the winter route follows the John Muir Trail toward Clark Point: at the fork, the gate on the upper John Muir Trail is closed, and the left fork drops to rejoin the Mist Trail just upstream of Vernal Fall, so the top of Vernal remains reachable when conditions cooperate. Nevada Fall does not: with the stair and the upper John Muir Trail both closed, there is no winter walking route to its brink, and the corridor's winter ambitions should end at Vernal. Go for the moving water and the empty canyon, keep expectations at that altitude, and read the Mist Trail guide for the rest of the year's version.

Hetch Hetchy, the winter secret

The best pure winter hiking in Yosemite is in its lowest corner. Hetch Hetchy sits below four thousand feet in the park's northwest, low enough that most winter weeks its trails are simply dry, or briefly white and then dry again. Lookout Point, about 2.8 miles round trip with 500 feet of gain from near the entrance station, buys a panorama of the reservoir and Wapama Falls for an hour and a half of walking. The main event, the Wapama Falls trail from O'Shaughnessy Dam, about five miles round trip of rolling granite ledge along the north shore, is a legitimate all-winter day hike: the falls begin returning with the first big rains, the south-facing trail takes what sun exists, and the valley that holds a hundred quiet visitors in July holds a dozen in January.

Two winter caveats. The access road is gated on posted hours, roughly sunrise to sunset, so the day has a hard frame; and snow can close the road entirely for days after a storm, so check conditions before making the drive. It is an hour and three quarters from Yosemite Valley, which is exactly why it is empty.

Wawona and the sequoias on foot

The south end of the park keeps two winter walks. The Wawona Meadow Loop, a flat 3.5 miles around the old meadow and golf course, stays walkable nearly all winter at Wawona's 4,000 feet, collecting snow just often enough to be worth timing. And the Mariposa Grove becomes, in my view, a better trip in winter than in July: the grove road closes to vehicles at the end of November, the shuttle stops, and the grove goes quiet. You walk up the closed road or the Washburn Trail, about two miles and a few hundred feet either way, then the Grizzly Giant Loop's two miles among snow-loaded sequoias, and you will meet more coyote tracks than people. Firm snow is boots-and-cleats; fresh snow is snowshoes; the grove guide has the winter section. Sequoias under snow are the best thing this park does in the cold months, and almost nobody sees it.

When the boots stop working

Above the Valley rim the season belongs to snowshoes and skis, and the line between that world and this article is the snow line itself. The marked winter routes out of Badger Pass, most famously the roughly seven-mile round trip to Dewey Point on the Valley's edge, are the way to stand on the winter rim, and the ranger-led snowshoe walks are the gentlest possible introduction. All of it, along with the chain rules that govern every drive in this piece, lives in the winter guide. The one crossover worth repeating here: snowshoes are for deep fresh snow, and on the packed, icy trails of the Valley floor they are worse than boots with cleats. Match the tool to the surface, not to the season.

Common questions

Can you actually hike in Yosemite in winter? Yes. The Valley floor's trails stay open all season, Columbia Rock is a real 1,000-foot climb that melts out between storms, the Vernal Fall footbridge stays reachable by its winter route, and Hetch Hetchy and Wawona hike nearly all winter at their lower elevations.

Is the Mist Trail open in winter? The granite stair beside Vernal Fall closes, along with the John Muir Trail between Clark Point and the top of Nevada Fall, because of falling ice. The winter route runs to the Vernal Fall footbridge and, conditions allowing, to the top of Vernal via Clark Point. Nevada Fall has no winter walking route.

Is the Four Mile Trail open in winter? No. It closes with the first lasting snow, typically into May, occasionally reopening its lowest miles to a gate below Union Point in lean spells.

Do you need snowshoes or just boots? On the Valley floor, Hetch Hetchy, and Wawona: boots, with traction cleats in your pocket for ice. Snowshoes earn their weight above the rim, out of Badger Pass, and in the Mariposa Grove after fresh snow.

What is the best winter hike in Yosemite? For effort against reward: Columbia Rock. For solitude and open trail: Wapama Falls at Hetch Hetchy. For the sight nobody expects: the walk up the closed road to the sequoias of the Mariposa Grove.

When does winter hiking season end? Gradually, from below. The floor is spring by April while the Mist Trail stair and the high trails shed their closures with the ice, and the Four Mile Trail waits for May. In between, this list and the March guide overlap on purpose.

The honest pitch

Winter subtracts most of the trail map and every crowd, and the trade is better than it reads. Walk the floor with cleats in your pocket, climb to Columbia Rock on the sunny wall, take the winter route to Vernal's footbridge, spend one day at Hetch Hetchy and one among snowbound sequoias, and you will have done five hikes that July cannot offer at any price. Watch the conditions page like a local, turn around when the ice says so, and be out of the shade by four. The park does the rest.

The trailheads are short; the daylight is shorter

Every walk on this list is governed by a winter sun that quits before five, which makes sleeping close the difference between hiking and commuting. Winter is also the season the rooms actually exist: a search on your dates will show in-park and gateway beds at prices July does not believe in.
