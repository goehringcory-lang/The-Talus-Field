The map goes grey somewhere past the last gateway town, the blue dot stops moving, and the group text about where to meet for lunch never sends. Later, at a trailhead on Tioga Road, somebody wants to check the smoke forecast, or the Half Dome lottery result that was supposed to arrive last night, and the phone in their hand is a camera with a flashlight. It surprises almost everyone the first time, because the question "is there cell service in Yosemite" gets a shrug on most websites and a marketing line from the rest. The Park Service publishes a carrier-by-carrier coverage table, and it is more specific than the shrug. This is that table in plain language, with the places you can find Wi-Fi, the handful of things on a trip that genuinely need a signal, and how to set up a phone so that it is useful with no bars at all.

The short answer

There is usable service on the three national carriers in the east end of Yosemite Valley, and very little anywhere else. That east end is the developed part of the Valley: Yosemite Village and the visitor center, the Ahwahnee, Curry Village, the Pines campgrounds. Outside it, coverage is a list of specific spots that depends on which carrier you pay, and the list is short. The park's own summary, on its Frequently Asked Questions page, is the sentence to plan around: "Cell service is available with some carriers in some areas, but is limited. Do not expect to have reliable service around the park."

Two consequences follow. First, anything that has to happen on a phone (a lottery result, a reservation confirmation, a route, a forecast) should be done or downloaded before the signal drops, which on every road in is well before the entrance station. Second, the plan for an emergency cannot be "we will call": the park says call or text 911, and the honest reading of the coverage table is that on most trails and all of the high country, neither will connect.

The Park Service's coverage table, carrier by carrier

The National Park Service keeps an Internet Access and Cell Service page for Yosemite that lists coverage by carrier and location. Its last stated update was April 16, 2024, so treat it as the park's best description rather than a live map, and expect the real signal to be a little worse than the list on a crowded weekend. What it says, condensed:

  • AT&T: voice and 4G LTE data in Yosemite Valley and at Glacier Point, "primarily in eastern Yosemite Valley," with FirstNet. At Tuolumne Meadows, "no cell service."
  • T-Mobile: 4G LTE and voice in Yosemite Valley, "primarily in eastern Yosemite Valley." That is the whole entry.
  • Verizon: the longest list. 4G LTE and voice in Yosemite Valley (again, primarily the east end), Foresta and Wawona; 4G and voice at Crane Flat; 4G LTE data and voice in El Portal, marked "limited and unreliable coverage"; and at Tuolumne Meadows, "cell service is not reliable. 4G LTE may be available."

Read the gaps as carefully as the entries. Tioga Road between Crane Flat and Tuolumne Meadows does not appear for any carrier. Neither does Hetch Hetchy, the Mariposa Grove, Glacier Point Road below the point itself, or any trail. Absence from the table is not proof of silence, but it is the park telling you not to count on anything there, and it matches what the high road is like in practice: essentially dead from Crane Flat to Lee Vining, which the park's own Tuolumne note confirms by naming Lee Vining, outside the park, as "the nearest reliable cell and traditional phone service." The same note adds that pay phones at Tuolumne "may not be available," so the old fallback is not one to plan on either.

One discrepancy is worth knowing about, because you will meet it. The park concessioner's travel-tips page says signals are "typically strongest in Yosemite Valley near the Visitor Center, Tuolumne Meadows, Crane Flat, and El Portal." On the Valley, Crane Flat and El Portal, that agrees with the Park Service, carrier permitting. On Tuolumne Meadows, it does not: the Park Service lists no service there for AT&T and unreliable service for Verizon. When the two disagree, plan on the Park Service's version, which is the pessimistic one, and be pleased if a bar appears at the store.

Where the signal drops on the way in

Every approach loses service before the gate. The entrances guide puts it as a rule rather than a warning: cell service dies well before the park boundary on every road in. On Highway 140, Mariposa is the last town, and El Portal's "limited and unreliable" Verizon coverage is the last flicker before Arch Rock. On Highway 41, Oakhurst is the last town, then nothing through the South Entrance until the Wawona pocket, which the table lists for Verizon only. On Highway 120 from the west, Groveland is the last town, and after the Big Oak Flat entrance there is Crane Flat for Verizon and nothing else until the Valley. From the east over Tioga Pass, Lee Vining is the last signal for the entire length of the road.

The practical translation is a rule about the last town: whatever needs a network, do it in the last town. Send the meeting plan, screenshot the reservation, load the route, refresh the forecast. A room in one of the gateway towns is the last reliable Wi-Fi you will see; inside the park you will be back on a network only in the few places listed next.

Wi-Fi: who gets it, and where

The Park Service lists three kinds of internet access inside the park, and none of them is a hotspot you can lean on for real work.

  • Hotel guests. Internet access is available to registered overnight guests at the Ahwahnee, Yosemite Valley Lodge and Curry Village. The concessioner adds the caveat that matters: bandwidth "is limited to light use, such as checking email." Do not plan to join a video call from a Curry Village tent cabin. The park's page also lists the Wawona Hotel, which has been closed since December 2024 for a structural assessment; the in-park lodging guide has the current state of every property.
  • The two county libraries. Mariposa County runs small branches in Yosemite Valley and in Wawona, with public computers and Wi-Fi. The catch is the hours. The county's own listing has the Yosemite branch, on Village Drive in Yosemite Village, open Tuesday and Wednesday from 11 am to 6 pm, and the Wawona branch open three days a week outside summer and Monday through Saturday between Memorial Day and Labor Day. Small-branch hours change; check the county page before you drive to one.
  • Degnan's Kitchen in Yosemite Village and El Portal Market, outside the Arch Rock entrance, where the park says free Wi-Fi is available. Degnan's is the one public network in the Valley that depends on neither a room key nor a library schedule, and it sits inside the east-end pocket where the carriers work anyway.

Nothing on that list is in the high country, at Glacier Point, in the Mariposa Grove, or at Hetch Hetchy, and the Tuolumne Meadows Lodge and White Wolf are not on the park's internet list at all. If a trip depends on being reachable, it is a Valley trip in a hotel, and even then the honest expectation is email.

The five things on a Yosemite trip that actually need a signal

Most of a visit needs no network. The visitors who are miserable about coverage are usually the ones who did not sort out these five before the last town.

1. The Half Dome daily lottery. The permit is required seven days a week whenever the cables are up, and the daily lottery runs on a two-day clock: you apply two days before your hiking date, between midnight and 4 pm Pacific, and the park says results go out "late that night." To hike Saturday you apply Thursday and learn Thursday night. If Thursday night finds you at a campsite in Tuolumne Meadows or on the west end of the Valley, the email that tells you whether you are hiking will not arrive until you are somewhere with service. Apply from the last town or from the east end of the Valley, and be in the same kind of place when the result is due. The Half Dome piece covers the permit system in full, including why Clouds Rest needs none of this.

2. The entrance pass and every confirmation. The park does not take cash at the gate; it takes cards and mobile payment, and a digital America the Beautiful pass bought on recreation.gov "can be saved to a mobile device and used immediately." Saved is the word. A pass that lives in an email you have to open at the kiosk is a pass you cannot show at Arch Rock. The same goes for the campground reservation, the lodging confirmation, the wilderness-permit reservation and the YARTS ticket: screenshot each one, or save it to the phone's wallet or files, in the last town. The wilderness centers, where a reserved permit is actually issued, keep 8 am to 5 pm hours, and the ranger at the desk wants to see the reservation, not watch you try to load it.

3. Driving directions. The park's FAQ warns that "GPS units often provide incorrect driving directions in and around Yosemite" and tells you to use its own written directions instead. The failure has two halves: a routing app that loses the network can keep navigating only if the map is already on the phone, and even then it may route you onto a road that is closed for the season. Download the offline map area for the whole park and the gateway corridor before you leave home, and decide which entrance to use before you leave rather than letting the phone pick.

4. Conditions, smoke and traffic. The things you would like to check from inside the park are exactly the things that need a network: the road status, the air-quality forecast on fire.airnow.gov that the park points to during a fire, the day's weather, the parking situation. Check them in the last town and once more from the Village if you pass through. The park also runs a text-alert line for Valley traffic, text ynptraffic to 333111, and a text needs a signal like anything else; sign up before the canyon, and expect the alerts to arrive in bursts when you next find coverage. The Park Bulletin and the conditions board on this site are built for that last-town check, and the smoke-season piece has the decision tree for a trip that meets a fire.

5. Meeting up. A group that splits at the trailhead cannot text its way back together. Agree on a place and a time before anyone walks off, and make the place a shuttle stop or a building, not "the meadow."

Emergencies without a bar

The park's instruction is plain: "If you have an emergency, call or text 911. Ambulance service is available 24 hours per day." The Yosemite Medical Clinic, at 209/372-4637, sits in the Valley between Yosemite Village and the Ahwahnee, and the park's general line is 209/372-0200. All of that assumes a connection, and on the coverage table above a connection exists in the east end of the Valley, in a handful of pockets, and nowhere on a trail that the park will vouch for. So the emergency plan has to be made for the case where the phone does nothing.

Satellite from the phone. Apple's Emergency SOS via satellite works on iPhone 14 or later in the United States, requires being outside with "a clear view of the sky and horizon," and is free for two years after the phone's activation, with terms after that set by Apple. Some recent Android phones offer a comparable satellite SOS; check the maker's support page for your model rather than assuming. On a Yosemite trail, "a clear view of the sky" is a real constraint: it is available on a dome, a meadow or a river bar, and not under a forest canopy or at the bottom of a slot like Tenaya Canyon. If your phone has the feature, test the demo mode at home, and know that it is a way to summon help, not a way to text the group.

A satellite messenger. Backpackers and anyone doing a long day out of the Valley should consider a dedicated two-way satellite communicator, sold by several makers with a subscription. It lets you send "running late, all fine" as well as "send help," which is most of what a family at home actually wants. The first-backpacking-trip piece covers the rest of the wilderness kit.

The old system. Tell someone your route and your turnaround time, and tell them when to worry. A search starts when someone who knows the plan reports that you are overdue, not when a phone goes silent, so that someone should be outside the park with a working phone and a written plan. On the trail, the fastest call for help is often another hiker walking out to a road, and the rangers at the wilderness centers, the visitor center and the entrance stations are the people to reach.

The offline kit: what to load before the last town

A phone with no signal is still the best map, guidebook and camera you are carrying, provided the things it needs were put on it while it had a network.

  • An offline map area in whichever maps app you use, drawn large enough to cover the gateway corridor and the whole park, Tioga Road included. This is the single item that prevents the most trouble, and it takes two minutes on the hotel Wi-Fi the night before.
  • The National Park Service app, with Yosemite downloaded. The park's own description is the reason to bother: you "can download content from entire parks for offline use," which gets you the park map, the shuttle stops, the trail descriptions and the program calendar without a bar.
  • The current Yosemite Guide, the park's free newspaper, as a PDF from the park website; the paper copy handed out at the entrance station never needs a signal, so keep it on the dashboard.
  • Screenshots of every reservation, permit and pass, and a photograph of the campsite number and the parking spot when you leave the car.
  • The Field Guide's offline download, if you carry it: the guide was built offline-first because the park mostly is, and the one tap that downloads the map, the hikes and the entries is meant to be pressed on a network, before Oakhurst or Mariposa or Groveland falls behind you.

Then turn on airplane mode with location left on: a phone hunting for a network it cannot find runs its battery down, the offline map works without the radio, and the cold nights at elevation are hard enough on a battery already. Carry the battery pack you would carry anyway, and treat the paper map from the gate as the copy that cannot run out.

Region by region, what to expect

Yosemite Valley, east end. The best coverage in the park, on all three carriers, and the only place the park lists T-Mobile at all. The Village, the visitor center, Curry Village, the Pines campgrounds and the Ahwahnee are inside it, and so is Degnan's Kitchen with its public Wi-Fi. It is still a deep canyon with a summer crowd sharing the towers, so a bar in the Village is not a promise of one at Happy Isles.

Yosemite Valley, west end. Tunnel View, Bridalveil Fall, El Capitan Meadow and Valley View are past the developed pocket, which is what the park's "primarily in eastern Yosemite Valley" qualifier describes: expect the signal to fade as you drive west, and to be unreliable at the viewpoints where everyone is trying to post the same photograph.

Glacier Point. Listed for AT&T only, at the point itself; nothing on the road, at Washburn Point, or on the Sentinel Dome and Taft Point trails is listed for anyone. Plan the sunset rendezvous before you leave the Valley.

Wawona and the Mariposa Grove. Wawona is a Verizon pocket, with the county library for Wi-Fi on its posted days. The Mariposa Grove, its shuttle and its trails are not listed. When a fire is burning near Wawona, as one was in September 2026, the park points visitors to fire.airnow.gov for smoke forecasts, and that is a page to load in Oakhurst.

Crane Flat, Foresta and El Portal. Verizon spots only. Crane Flat matters because it is the junction where the drive turns onto Tioga Road: for a Verizon phone it is the last signal until Lee Vining, and for the other carriers the Valley was.

Tioga Road and Tuolumne Meadows. Nothing reliable for anyone, by the park's own account. Tenaya Lake, Olmsted Point, the Cathedral Lakes and Lembert Dome trailheads, the Tuolumne campground and the wilderness center are all off the network for practical purposes. This is where a group splits up on a whim and cannot regroup, where a hiker on Clouds Rest expects to text a summit photograph and cannot, and where the Half Dome lottery email sits unread. Any Tuolumne day plan has to assume all of this.

Hetch Hetchy. Not listed for any carrier, and the road is open only from sunrise to sunset. For the phone, treat it as trail country.

The trails, all of them. No trail appears in the table. A summit or a ridge may catch a bar from a distant tower on a good day; the bottom of a canyon will not. Treat any signal above the Valley floor as a bonus and never as part of the plan.

The practical version

Do the phone work in the last town: send the plan, screenshot the reservations and the pass, download the offline map, the park app and the guide, apply for the lottery and know where you will be when the result comes. Assume nothing works on Tioga Road, on Glacier Point Road, at Hetch Hetchy or on any trail, and assume the west end of the Valley is worse than the east. If someone needs to reach you, you are staying at a Valley hotel and checking email, not taking calls. Carry the paper Guide from the gate, a battery pack, and a written plan left with someone outside the park, and for a long day or any night out, a satellite messenger or a phone that can reach one. Then put the phone in airplane mode and look up. The park is not withholding a signal out of spite. It is a granite trough three thousand feet deep with mountains on every side, and the towers were never going to win.
