Stand at Tunnel View with the park map open and you can read most of the Valley off it in a minute: El Capitan on the left, Bridalveil Fall on the right, Half Dome at the far end, Cathedral Rocks along the south wall. The names feel as permanent as the granite. Most of the landmark names on that map date from 1851 or later, and many were chosen by men who had come into the Valley that year to remove the people who lived there, and several of the translations that still circulate were guesses when they were first printed. The word on the park entrance sign is the clearest case. For most of a century it was said to mean "grizzly bear." It almost certainly does not.

What follows is the record as the sources give it: who named each landmark, when, the earlier name, and how much weight each translation can bear. The main witness is Lafayette Bunnell of the Mariposa Battalion, whose Discovery of the Yosemite (1880) is candid about where he was guessing. The corrections come from linguists and from the park's own naturalists in Yosemite Nature Notes.

A name given by a campfire vote

The Mariposa Battalion was a volunteer force sanctioned by the State of California in 1851. The Park Service describes its purpose plainly: to rid the area of the perceived threat of Indians, after thousands of gold rush miners had pushed into the foothills and some Indians struck back by raiding a trading post, killing several miners. Its companies entered the Valley in March 1851. Bunnell put the date at "about the 21st of March." When they arrived, the Park Service says, they systematically burned villages and food supplies and forced men, women, and children away from their homes.

That first evening in the Valley, by Bunnell's account, the talk around the fire turned to what to call the place. Others offered romantic, foreign and scriptural names. Bunnell argued for "an American name," saying an Indian name would be better than an imported one, and proposed "Yo-sem-i-ty," the name the battalion used for the band it was pursuing, so that "the name of the tribe of Indians which we met leaving their homes in this valley, perhaps never to return, would be perpetuated." One man objected that the Indians' name should not be honored at all; another suggested Paradise Valley instead. A Texan named John O'Neal called a voice vote, and Bunnell records that Yo-sem-i-ty was "almost unanimously adopted."

The spelling changed within a year. Bunnell credits Lieutenant Moore of the U.S. Army, reporting on an expedition to the Valley in 1852, with replacing the final "y" with an "e," "no doubt thinking the use of e more scholarly." The new spelling stuck. So did the name, despite a challenge: James Hutchings, who visited in 1855 and whose energetic publishing did much to advertise the Valley, called it "Yo-Hamite" for some time, and a San Francisco minister proposed "Yo-Amite." Bunnell supplied the facts for newspaper editorials defending the original, and in 1859 Hutchings published Bunnell's account in his own magazine and conceded the point.

What "Yosemite" means

At the campfire, Bunnell writes, the meaning of the word "was not generally known to our battalion." The battalion's commander, Major James Savage, a trader who spoke several of the region's languages, told them it signified "a full-grown grizzly bear," and that the name had been given to Tenaya's band "because of their lawless and predatory character." Savage also admitted that he "could not readily understand" Tenaya or the Indian guide. Bunnell later learned from Savage that Tenaya rejected the word as a name for the Valley but accepted it for his band.

The grizzly reading lasted a long time because serious people kept it alive. In 1921 the anthropologist A. L. Kroeber called "Yosemite" a corruption of the Miwok word for grizzly bear, reasoning from the Miwok division of people into two halves, one of which the grizzly bear belonged to. C. Hart Merriam, who had described that division in 1917, agreed. As late as July 1936 a park naturalist, James Cole, weighed the evidence in Nature Notes and judged that theory the strongest of the several he examined. His article, with its doubts left in, is in this site's Nature Notes archive.

The linguists went the other way. In 1919 S. A. Barrett broke the word into "yo-he," to kill, and a word for "people." In 1955 Madison Beeler, a Berkeley linguist, set out the case in the journal Names, working from Lucy Freeland's 1951 grammar of Sierra Miwok. Freeland had recorded two similar words: one for bear, and one defined as "the Killers." Beeler argued the second was the source. The word for bear lacks the initial "y" sound; the other fits Miwok grammar exactly, a root meaning "to kill," a suffix meaning "one who," and a collective plural. Freeland's note, which Beeler quotes, describes it as "the name which was applied by the neighboring Miwok to the dwellers in Yosemite Valley." Sylvia Broadbent's 1964 study of the Southern Sierra Miwok language supports the same reading.

Craig Bates summed it up in Yosemite Nature Notes in 1978: the word translates as "they are killers," on the evidence of fluent Southern Sierra Miwok speakers. So "Yosemite" was what outsiders called Tenaya's people, and Bunnell, also an outsider, gave it to the Valley. One loose variant should be set aside. A 1977 revision of the booklet Yosemite Indians rendered the word "some among them are killers." Dan Anderson, who edits the yosemite.ca.us online library, points out that nothing in the word means "some," and Bates's own 1978 article gives only "they are killers." Anderson also answers the idea that "killers" referred to the battalion: the word was in Miwok use before the battalion existed.

Ahwahnee, and the people who lived there

The Valley already had a name. Tenaya told the battalion, through interpreters, "I am the descendant of an Ah-wah-ne-chee chief. ... The valley was then called Ah-wah-nee. Ah-wah-ne-chee signifies the dwellers in Ahwahnee." The battalion's Captain Boling replied, in Bunnell's account, that the older name was "all right enough for history or poetry" but that "we could not now change the name Yosemite."

Bunnell tried to learn what Ahwahnee meant. Tenaya, he writes, "by the motion of his hands, indicated depth," while plucking grass and holding it up. "From these 'signs' I inferred that it must mean the deep grassy valley. Still, it may not mean that." Later writers dropped the caution and kept the guess. Bates traced the word to a Southern Miwok stem for "mouth," giving "the big mouth" or "place of the big mouth," and quoted a 1940 letter by a park naturalist reporting that Chief Lemee, a local Indian, "explained Ahwahnee as big mouth." The Park Service now gives the meaning as "gaping mouth-like place," and says the people who lived there called themselves Ahwahneechee. Bates also recorded what Miwok speakers remembered in 1978: the word as a name for the Yosemite people, and possible senses such as "place you go to pound acorn." His conclusion is worth keeping beside any translation in this piece: when searching for the meaning of a native term, "one can never be too careful, nor ultimately correct."

Who those people were is part of the story too. Bunnell repeats the battalion's belief that Tenaya's band had been formed from refugees of other tribes, and Tenaya's own account that his mother was a Mono woman. The Park Service describes nineteenth-century Yosemite as home to Southern Miwok people who had been joined by Mono Lake Kootzaduka'a, Chukchansi Yokuts, Western Mono and former Mission Indians. Its summary of what followed is blunt: by 1910, over 90 percent of the original Ahwahneechee inhabitants were dead or missing. The park today recognizes seven traditionally associated tribes and groups: the American Indian Council of Mariposa County (the Southern Sierra Miwuk Nation), the Bishop Paiute Tribe, the Bridgeport Indian Colony, the Mono Lake Kootzaduka'a Tribe of California and Nevada, the North Fork Rancheria of Mono Indians of California, the Picayune Rancheria of the Chukchansi Indians, and the Tuolumne Band of Me-Wuk Indians. "Miwok," the Park Service notes, translates as "people."

El Capitan, Half Dome and the domes

El Capitan. Bunnell heard the cliff called Tote-ack-ah-noo-la by the battalion's scouts and by Tenaya, and the scouts translated it as "the Rock Chief" or "the Captain." Asked why a cliff would be called "he," one scout took Bunnell to a spot where a stain on the face resembled a man's head. The battalion adopted the Spanish "El Capitan," Bunnell writes, to please its mission interpreters. He rejected a claim then in print that the name meant "Crane Mountain." A different reading, "measuring-worm stone," comes from the ethnographer Stephen Powers and a story about a measuring worm that rescued two children stranded when the rock grew. That is the version park naturalist Richard Hartesveldt printed in his 1955 Yosemite Valley Place Names. The two translations do not reconcile, and neither has a modern linguist's endorsement in these sources.

Half Dome. Bunnell says the battalion named the North Dome, the South Dome and the Half Dome "from their localities and peculiar configuration," and records a sergeant's story that a man named Spencer looked at the "mountain with one side split off" and "called it a 'half dome.'" The labels then shifted: the battalion's South Dome became Sentinel Dome, and Half Dome itself was for years called South Dome. Bunnell gives the native name as Tis-sa-ack, "represented as meaning the 'Cleft Rock.'" The better-known story, in which Tis-sa-ack is a woman who quarreled with her husband and was turned to stone, her tears the dark streaks on the face, is the one Hartesveldt retold in 1955; he also noted an early trail leaflet's "Goddess of the Valley," a translation Bunnell had already found in Hutchings and called "more artistically imaginative than correct." For the climb itself, see our guide to hiking Half Dome.

North Dome and Royal Arches. Bunnell gives North Dome's native name as To-ko-ya, "the Basket." The arches beneath it were Scho-ko-ni, the movable shade over a baby's cradle. The English name came from a battalion member who belonged to the Masonic fraternity and called it "The Royal Arch"; it became the plural over time.

Three Brothers. This name records the campaign directly. Bunnell writes that the battalion brought in five captives near the base of these peaks, three of them sons of Tenaya, "one of whom was afterwards killed," and named the peaks for the coincidence. The native name, Kom-po-pai-zes, he connected to a resemblance between the peaks and the heads of frogs sitting up to leap, then added that "a literal translation is not desirable." The online edition's editor glosses the omitted meaning as a sexual one. The "mountains playing leap-frog" has, Hartesveldt wrote, no basis. Bunnell also describes Indians the battalion was chasing escaping into the Talus below the Three Brothers, the apron of broken rock that the site's piece on talus fields explains.

Clouds Rest. One of the few names Bunnell credits to his comrades. A party exploring Little Yosemite turned back when clouds settled on the mountain, a sign of the snowstorm that followed.

The waterfalls

Yosemite Falls. Bunnell named it, and says he followed the native usage: the fall was called "Choo-look" or "Scholook," which in this case signified "The Fall."

Bridalveil Fall. The Ahwahneechee name was Pohono. Bunnell could not get a literal meaning at first and later accepted "a daily puffing wind," citing Stephen Cunningham, and it matches what he saw the fall do: an air current up the canyon threw the water "back and up in rocket-like jets." Hutchings's "Spirit of the Evil Wind" Bunnell judged "more artistically imaginative than correct." The people who summered in that drainage were the Po-ho-no-chee, "the dwellers in Po-ho-no." The English name was suggested by Warren Baer, editor of the Mariposa Democrat, on a visit with Bunnell; Hartesveldt dates it to August 1856 and notes that Hutchings also claimed it. The site's waterfalls guide covers when each fall is worth the trip.

Vernal and Nevada falls. Both are Bunnell's, given during the battalion's spring stay. The Merced up to Vernal was Yan-o-pah, "the water cloud" or "little cloud," because the trail passed through the spray; Vernal Fall took the English word for spring because the spray, sun and new grass there "suggested the sensation of spring." Nevada Fall's native name was Yo-wy-we, which Bunnell connected to the twist the water takes over an obstructing rock; Hartesveldt renders it "twisted water." Bunnell chose "Nevada," Spanish for snowy, because the white water looked to him like an avalanche and because the fall was nearest the Sierra Nevada.

Illilouette Fall. Bunnell gives the native name of the creek as Too-lool-lo-we-ack and declared the spelling "Illeuette" "not Indian, and is, therefore, meaningless and absurd." He called a literal translation of the original "inadmissable," mocked the published rendering "The Beautiful," and proposed "Glacier Fall" instead. Nobody adopted it.

Sentinel, Cathedral, Glacier Point and the rim

Sentinel Rock. The sources disagree. Bunnell writes that the battalion's mission Indians figuratively called Half Dome "the Sentinel," and that the rock now bearing the name was Loya, which he took to be a corruption of the Spanish olla, a water pot, from its shape. Hartesveldt credits the English name to the Whitney survey party, for the rock's tower-like position.

Photo: OMCV / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)

Cathedral Rocks and Spires. Bunnell gives the native name for the rocks and spires together as Poo-see-na-chuc-ka, "Mouse-proof Rocks," from their resemblance to the acorn caches. Hartesveldt preferred "large acorn cache" and said the "mouse-proof" sense was not verified elsewhere. Hutchings named the spires in September 1862 because they looked like the towers of a Gothic cathedral. Seen from the west the same rocks were once called the Three Graces, a usage Hartesveldt quotes from Galen Clark; Bunnell never learned the meaning of their native name, Ko-sü-kong.

Glacier Point. Its naming is unknown, Hartesveldt wrote. Bunnell gives the native name as Pa-til-le-ma and says he cannot translate it. Hartesveldt doubted the battalion chose the English name, since few of its members would have known enough about glaciers to see the point in it. A guidebook translation of another supposed native name as "Great Rock of the Elk" he called illogical, because elk did not live in the region. More on the overlook is in our Glacier Point guide.

Washburn Point. Hartesveldt wrote that it was "undoubtedly" named for Albert Washburn of Wawona, one of the men who took over Clark's and Moore's station. Washburn Slide, a talus slope below Silver Strand Falls, carries the surname too; Hartesveldt does not say for whom.

Mirror Lake. Bunnell's. The scouts pointed to the morning reflection and said "Look at Wai-ack," interpreted as "Water Rock," the name for both the lake and the mountain it reflected. Others objected that all still water is a mirror; Bunnell answered that he would move the name if anyone showed him a better pool. The mountain is now Mount Watkins, which Hartesveldt says was named for the photographer Carleton Watkins.

Tenaya Lake and the high country

Photo: Michael Hogarth / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)

The most uncomfortable naming in the record happened in the high country. In early June 1851, by Bunnell's dating, the battalion surprised the last of Tenaya's band camped on the shore of a lake and led them out of the mountains. Bunnell proposed naming the lake for the chief, and Captain Boling agreed it was "a kind of justice." When Bunnell told Tenaya, the chief pointed to the glistening peaks at the head of the lake and said, "It already has a name; we call it Py-we-ack." Bunnell understood it as "the river of glistening rocks." He also writes that Tenaya's "countenance fell," that he walked away, and that his face said he thought the naming "no equivalent for the loss of his territory." The lake, the creek and the canyon below it all carry Tenaya's name now; Py-we-ack survives only in the record. The lake is now a roadside stop on the drive over Tioga Road.

The peaks above Tuolumne took their names a decade later from a different kind of expedition. The California Geological Survey under Josiah Whitney named Mount Dana on June 28, 1863, for the Yale geologist James Dwight Dana, according to the record its party left on the summit, and Mount Hoffmann that year for the survey's own topographer, Charles F. Hoffmann. Whitney's 1870 guidebook explains Mount Lyell as named for the British geologist Sir Charles Lyell. Whitney also observed that the names given by early white visitors "have entirely replaced the native ones." On the main Valley landmarks, the record above bears that out.

Where to see the names in place

The Valley's names are easiest to read where most of them are in one view. Tunnel View lines up El Capitan, Bridalveil Fall and Half Dome. Hartesveldt places the battalion's first view of the Valley at Old Inspiration Point, on the south rim about a mile southeast of Inspiration Point, which sits just south of the Wawona Tunnel. Ahwahnee Meadow, the largest meadow on the Valley floor, between Yosemite Village and the Royal Arches, keeps the older name; Hartesveldt wrote that it may first have belonged to this meadow alone.

Behind the Yosemite Museum, the Park Service maintains the Indian Village of Ahwahnee on the site of what it describes as the largest Indian village in Yosemite Valley. Members of the local American Indian community still use it for ceremonies and gatherings. In summer and fall the museum hosts demonstrations by Native artists from the associated tribes; our piece on Connecting to Traditions has the schedule.

For the sources themselves, Bunnell's Discovery of the Yosemite, Hartesveldt's Yosemite Valley Place Names, Bates's 1978 article and Beeler's 1955 note are all transcribed in the yosemite.ca.us online library. In this record, the translations that sound most poetic are usually the ones with the weakest sources.
