Glacier Point is on every first visitor's list, and it fails more of those visitors than any other place in the park. Not the view, which does exactly what the photographs promise. The plan. The road is closed for roughly half the year. In summer the parking lot at the end of it fills by mid-morning, and the Park Service now turns the overflow around and sends it to a shuttle eleven miles back down the road. And the drive is about an hour each way from Yosemite Valley, a number most people learn in the car. This is the standing guide: what is at the top, how the road works and when it is open, the shuttle rule that governs a summer visit, the walks that start from the road, the bus up, and the ski route that is the only way in from December to spring.

What is at the top

The Park Service puts the overlook at 3,214 feet above Curry Village, and the number is the whole experience. You are not looking at Half Dome from below, the way the Valley shows it, or from the side, the way Tunnel View does. You are level with it, close enough to read the joints in its face, with the Merced canyon dropping away underneath you and Vernal and Nevada Falls stacked in it like two steps of a staircase. Yosemite Falls is across the Valley. East, past Half Dome, the Clark Range and the high peaks hold snow into early summer.

From the parking area a paved, wheelchair-accessible trail of about 300 yards leads to the point, a ten- or fifteen-minute walk on a gentle grade. There are restrooms, a stone amphitheater, a snack bar and gift shop in season, and on the terrace below the shop a bronze relief model of the country you are looking at, worth two minutes so the peaks have names. Drinking water is a bottle-filling station that the park describes as available from late spring to early fall, which means it may not be running in the first weeks of the season or the last; carry your own. This is the highest-return view per step in Yosemite, and the non-hiker's guide treats it as the anchor of a day for exactly that reason.

A naturalist's note on what you are seeing, because the view is an argument as well as a picture. The Valley below is U-shaped, flat-floored and straight-walled, and the waterfalls fall from side valleys that hang in the air far above the floor. Both are the signature of ice: a trunk glacier deepened the main canyon far faster than the side streams could cut down, and when it melted, the side valleys were left stranded on the rim with their creeks pouring out of them. The stepped Merced canyon under your feet, the giant staircase of the geologists, is the same story told in the canyon's own granite. Once you have seen the shape of the thing from up here you cannot unsee it from the floor.

The road, mile by mile

Glacier Point is about 30 miles and an hour from Yosemite Valley, and the same hour from Wawona. From the Valley you take the Wawona Road, which is Highway 41 inside the park, south for about 13 miles to the Chinquapin junction, then turn onto Glacier Point Road and follow it about 16 miles to the end. There is no regular shuttle between the Valley and the point: you drive, you ride the paid tour bus, or you walk up.

The road is the visit, not the approach to it. Badger Pass, the ski area, is five miles in. The McGurk Meadow trailhead follows, then the turn for Bridalveil Creek Campground, then the shared Sentinel Dome and Taft Point trailhead a couple of miles short of the end, then Washburn Point, whose lot sits just south of Glacier Point and whose view of Vernal and Nevada Falls is more direct than the point's own. Most people who drive straight through and complain about the parking never stopped at Washburn, which is the answer to the parking.

Two rules of the road. Vehicles longer than 30 feet and anything towing a trailer are not allowed past the Sentinel Dome parking lot, so a large RV ends its drive there. And there is no usable phone signal for most of the road, which means the conditions you check at breakfast are the ones you are carrying; the Park Bulletin is the place to check.

The season, in the park's own dates

The Park Service's standing language is that the road is open to cars "from approximately late May through October or November," and the standing language undersells how wide the window swings. The park publishes the dates, and over the twenty-six seasons from 2000 through 2025 Glacier Point Road opened as early as March 28 (2015) and as late as July 15 (2023, a construction year), with most openings falling in May. It did not open at all in 2022, when it was closed for a rehabilitation project. The closing is a weather call, made after the first significant snowfall: most closings land in November, the earliest on record in that span was October 31 (2003) and the latest was December 12 (2008), and in 2021 the road stayed open into the first week of December.

Plowing begins around April 15, and the park says plainly that it cannot predict the opening date even in late spring. So a trip booked for the first half of May is a coin toss on this road, and one booked for the second half of November is a coin toss the other way. In every season since 2000 in which the road opened at all, it was open by mid-July and stayed open through October. Two dates inside the season are fixed rather than forecast: overnight parking anywhere on the road is prohibited from October 15, which matters if you were planning to leave a car at the Four Mile trailhead, and the road closes to cars the moment the snow makes it, with no reopening promised. The early-season piece describes the first weeks after the gate comes up, when the water is off and the snack bar may be shut; the fall guide covers the other end, when the road is among the last of the high roads to close.

The summer rule: when the lot fills, you ride from Badger Pass

This is the part of a summer visit that the older guides, and until now this site, did not carry. The Park Service's Glacier Point page, updated in June 2026, sets it out: from mid-May to September, between 10 am and 4:30 pm, when the Glacier Point parking lot fills, rangers stop cars at the Badger Pass turnoff and have you park there and ride a free shuttle to Glacier Point and the trailheads along the road. If you reach the Badger Pass turnoff before 10 am or after 4:30 pm, you drive through to Glacier Point and every trailhead. Parking at Badger Pass takes RVs and trailers but not full-sized commercial buses.

The shuttle leaves Badger Pass every 20 minutes and stops at the Sentinel Dome and Taft Point trailhead, Washburn Point, and Glacier Point; on request or a flag-down it also stops at the McGurk Meadow, Ostrander Lake, and Mono Meadow trailheads. The first shuttle leaves Badger Pass at 10 am, the last leaves Badger Pass at 4:30 pm, and the last shuttle leaves Glacier Point at 5:30 pm, after which there is no shuttle of any kind. Three groups can drive to their destination at any hour regardless: vehicles displaying an accessibility placard, people holding a wilderness permit for a Glacier Point Road trailhead, and people with a campsite at Bridalveil Creek. The park adds that the details may change during the summer, so read its page in the week you are going, not this one.

The practical reading is simpler than the rule. A summer visit to Glacier Point has two good hours, early and late, and a shuttle in between. Arrive before ten and the day is yours, including the hikes. Arrive at noon on a July Saturday and plan on Badger Pass, a twenty-minute wait and a ride, and a last bus down at half past five that ends any idea of staying for the light. Arrive after 4:30 and you drive again, into the evening the lot was built for. The park frames the rule as mid-May to September. In October the same lot can fill for sunset with nobody managing it, and that is the case the next section is about.

The hour decides the rest

Sunrise is the clean answer in any month. The lot is empty, the light reaches the high peaks east of the Valley first and works down the walls in order, Half Dome before the Clark Range before the floor, and you are driving back down as the first shuttle of the day is loading at Badger Pass. The cost is an alarm set for the small hours and a rim that can run twenty degrees colder than the Valley.

Sunset is the answer people want, and it has a catch the summer rule does not solve. After 4:30 pm you can drive up, but the lot at the end of the road is finite and a sunset crowd can fill it in any month the road is open, with no shuttle to fall back on after 5:30 pm. So arrive with an hour to spare, and if the Glacier Point lot is full, take the Washburn Point lot a short way back and walk: the sunset on Half Dome is the same, the falls are more in view, and the crowd at the railing is a fraction. Then remember the drive down is an hour in the dark, on a road with deer on it.

Midday is for the hikes, which start from lots the shuttle serves; if you are riding it anyway, the Sentinel Dome and Taft Point lot is a stop.

The walks, from the point and from the road

The Park Service's own numbers, which are the ones to use. From the shared trailhead a couple of miles short of the end of the road, Sentinel Dome is 2 miles round trip with 400 feet of gain, one to two hours, and finishes on a bare granite cap with a 360-degree view that includes El Capitan, Half Dome, and Yosemite Falls; the park's one instruction is to stay off the dome in a thunderstorm. From the same lot, Taft Point is 2.2 miles round trip, with about 200 feet of climbing on the way back, through forest and meadow to a rim overlook that faces El Capitan and Yosemite Falls across the Valley. The Fissures beside Taft Point are joints in the granite, narrow cracks with drops the park puts at up to 2,000 feet, and a short railing guards the point itself and nothing else. The two trails join through the Pohono Trail into a loop of roughly five miles, which is the walk most first visitors should do on a day the Mist Trail is a queue. Pit toilets are at the trailhead lot. Pets and bicycles are not allowed on any of these trails.

Lower on the road, McGurk Meadow is 1.6 miles round trip and nearly flat, a wildflower walk in early summer to a historic cabin, and the same trailhead continues to Dewey Point at 7.6 miles round trip with 750 feet of gain, four to five hours, for a rim view from the west end of the Valley. Mono Meadow is 3 miles round trip and wet most of the summer, with a steep 900 feet to climb on the way back. Ostrander Lake is 12 miles round trip and 1,500 feet, a full day.

From the point itself, three trails go down. The Four Mile Trail is 4.8 miles one way to the Valley floor and a very steep descent, three to four hours down, about 3,200 feet, and there is no water anywhere on it; the loop that climbs it and comes down the Panorama Trail is the day hike this site rates highest in the park. The Panorama Trail is 8.2 miles one way past Illilouette Fall to the top of Nevada Fall and down the John Muir or Mist Trail, six to eight hours, with an 800-foot climb out of the Illilouette drainage that surprises people who expected a descent. The Pohono Trail runs 12.5 miles along the south rim to Tunnel View. All three are one-way problems: the tour bus sells one-way tickets up and does not pick up at the point, so the standard shape is bus up and walk down.

The bus, if you would rather not drive

The park concessioner runs the Glacier Point Tour whenever the road is open, and for the 2026 season it listed May 9 through October 12, departing the lobby entrance of Yosemite Valley Lodge at 8:30 am and 1:30 pm for a trip of about four hours that takes in Tunnel View on the way. Round trip was $72.50 for adults and $50.50 for children two to twelve, and the one-way ticket, the hiker's fare, was $28.50 for adults and $18.25 for children. The full-day Grand Tour, eight hours from the Lodge at 8 am, added the Mariposa Grove for $141 and $71. The prices are the concessioner's for one season and will move, so treat them as the scale rather than the quote. For a group with someone who cannot or would rather not drive a mountain road twice in a day, the bus is the right answer.

After dark

The Park Service lists Glacier Point and Washburn Point among its stargazing spots for the plain reason that they sit at 7,000 feet with an open sky to the south and the Valley's lights well below the rim. Summer brings astronomy programs at the point, run variously by the park, the Yosemite Conservancy, and the concessioner, some ticketed; the park's calendar and the current Yosemite Guide are the schedule, and the stargazing guide explains what the site's darkness is worth. The logistics are the same as sunset with the lights off: no shuttle after 5:30 pm, an hour down in the dark, and a coat you did not think you needed in July.

Winter: the road ends at Badger Pass

From roughly mid-December through March the first five miles of Glacier Point Road are plowed to Badger Pass, the ski area that has run there since 1935, and everything beyond it is closed to cars and groomed for skiing and snowshoeing. It is not possible to drive to Glacier Point in winter. What is possible is to ski the road, 10.5 miles one way, as a 21-mile day for strong cross-country skiers or as an overnight. The concessioner's Glacier Point Ski Hut, twenty bunks at the point that hosted guided overnight trips from December to March, did not open for the 2025-26 winter, which the concessioner attributes to a lack of power; check whether it has reopened before you plan around it. The alternative is camping, with a wilderness permit available at the Badger Pass Ranger Station without a reservation. Tire chains may be required on the plowed section from late fall into early spring, and a free Badger Pass shuttle runs from the Valley on the days the ski area operates. The winter guide puts this in the context of a season when the Valley is open and the rim is not.

Sleeping on the road

Bridalveil Creek Campground is the one place to spend the night on Glacier Point Road with a car. Sites have a fire ring, a picnic table, and a food locker, and sit near a bathroom with drinking water and flush toilets; reservations are through recreation.gov, and the park asks you to read a site's details there before booking because length limits vary. The reason to sleep here rather than in the Valley is the summer rule above: a Bridalveil Creek camper drives the road at any hour, sunrise and sunset included, without a shuttle in the way. The site's campground guide ranks it against the park's other twelve.

What used to be here

The stone amphitheater stands where two hotels did. In 1871 James McCauley won permission to build a toll trail from the base of Sentinel Rock to Glacier Point, the route that became the Four Mile Trail, and in 1873 the innkeeper Charles Peregoy put up the Mountain House at the overlook for the people who climbed it. The Glacier Point Hotel followed in 1917, with rooms for some two hundred guests, and the Park Service's own history allows that "there may have never been a better balcony view." Campfires built for the Mountain House's guests were pushed over the edge, and from Camp Curry the falling embers looked like a waterfall of fire. That became the Firefall, and the June 1934 issue of Yosemite Nature Notes, in the site's archive, has the assistant park naturalist's own account: a quarter cord of red fir bark burned down to coals near the overhanging rock, a call of "Let the fire fall" exchanged with Camp Curry at nine o'clock, and a stream of embers dropping some 1,400 feet to a ledge. The hotel was damaged by the snowpack of the winter of 1968 to 1969 and did not open that summer, the Firefall was cancelled for good, and on July 10, 1969 an electrical fire in the empty building, fed by the pile of fir bark left beside it, took the hotel, the Mountain House, and a stand of trees.

Three shapes of a Glacier Point day

The morning drive. Leave the Valley before first light, drive through the Badger Pass turnoff while it is unstaffed, park at the end of the road, walk the 300 yards, and stay at the railing longer than feels reasonable. Washburn Point on the way down, McGurk Meadow if it is June, and back in the Valley by lunch.

The hike day. Drive early or ride the shuttle to the Sentinel Dome and Taft Point lot, walk the loop, eat on the rim, and cover the last stretch to the point in the afternoon with your legs already loose. Or buy the one-way bus ticket and come down the Four Mile or the Panorama Trail on foot, which turns the drive into a descent and the descent into the day.

The sunset. Arrive after 4:30 pm with an hour in hand, take Washburn Point if the end of the road is full, and stay until the color has gone off Half Dome. Drive down slowly. If you want to do it without the parking anxiety at all, book a night at Bridalveil Creek and drive the road as if it were yours, because for that night it is. The trip-planner map will place the point, the trailhead, and the campground on the road for you.
