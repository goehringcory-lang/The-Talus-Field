Most visitors arrive at the Mariposa Grove with the same wrong plan: they intend to drive to the trees. You cannot, not in summer, and not since 2018. The parking is two miles down the hill by the South Entrance and a free shuttle covers the gap. Nearly everything else that goes wrong on a grove day follows from not knowing that one fact, so this piece starts there and works outward.

The geography, which is the whole problem

The grove sits in the park's southern corner, immediately inside the South Entrance on Highway 41. It is not near Yosemite Valley. From the Valley floor it is about an hour of driving each way, which is the single most underestimated number in this article.

Once you are through the entrance station you reach a roundabout almost at once, and the Mariposa Grove Welcome Plaza is the first right, less than a quarter mile in. That is where you park. The grove itself, what the Park Service calls the Arrival Area, is another two miles up Mariposa Grove Road, and in season that road is closed to private vehicles. A free shuttle covers the two miles. It is not relaxed on quiet days. It is the permanent arrangement the 2018 restoration created, for reasons covered below.

The shuttle

It is free, there is no ticket, and individual riders need no reservation. Buses run roughly every fifteen minutes and take about ten minutes to cover the two miles. They are wheelchair accessible, with lifts and tie-downs, and chair users board first.

The Park Service does not publish a hard date for the season. Its standing language is that the shuttle begins no earlier than April 15 and ends on or before November 30. That is a commitment to a window, not a promise about a day, and it exists because the opening depends on when the road is clear of snow.

There is one firm 2026 date, though it applies to other vehicles. Buses and vehicles over about twenty-one feet must reserve grove parking through recreation.gov, and that reservation window this year runs May 3 through November 30. It is a reasonable proxy for the operating season, because the reservations exist to manage a road the shuttle is running on. Treat it as an inference, not a timetable. Secondary sites publish hour-by-hour tables that disagree with each other, and none of them is a Park Service figure. Check the posted hours at the plaza when you arrive, and note the last downhill departure before you walk away from the stop. People miss it, and the walk down is two miles.

If you hold a disability placard

The road closure has an exception. A vehicle displaying a disability placard or plate may drive Mariposa Grove Road to the Arrival Area, and there is accessible parking at the top. The spaces are described by the Park Service as very limited, which in practice means a handful, so this is a privilege that works best early in the day. The Big Trees Loop at the top is paved and flat, which makes the grove one of the more rewarding accessible destinations in the park. The accessibility guide covers what the placard does and does not get you elsewhere in Yosemite.

Walking up instead

You can also walk from the plaza to the grove on the Washburn Trail, which climbs roughly two miles and a few hundred feet through mixed conifer forest before the first sequoias appear. There are two reasons to do it. First, the shuttle queue on an August afternoon can be long enough that walking is competitive. Second, on foot you watch the forest change as the sequoias appear.

The four walks, in order of ambition

The Big Trees Loop. Three tenths of a mile, paved and boardwalked, flat, wheelchair accessible, starting from the Arrival Area. It passes the Fallen Monarch, the toppled sequoia whose upturned root mass appears in one of the most reproduced photographs in the park's history, a US Cavalry troop and their horses drawn up along the trunk. If someone in your group cannot walk far, this is the loop that still gives them the grove.

The Grizzly Giant Loop. About two miles with a few hundred feet of climbing, and the one to do if you are only doing one. It takes in the Bachelor and Three Graces, the Grizzly Giant itself, and the California Tunnel Tree. Two hours is a comfortable allowance, including time spent stopping at the trees.

The Guardians Loop. Six and a half miles or so into the upper grove, with something over a thousand feet of gain. This is where the crowds thin to almost nothing, and where the Faithful Couple, the Clothespin Tree, and the Telescope Tree are. Give it however long you would normally give a six-mile hike, then add an hour.

The Mariposa Grove Trail to Wawona Point. Roughly seven miles round trip from the Arrival Area with a similar amount of climbing, ending at an overlook above the Wawona basin. It is the full day, and the only one of the four that ends with a view instead of a tree.

Distances and gains published for the upper-grove trails vary between sources by enough to matter on a hot afternoon, and some trail-aggregator sites use loop names that do not appear on Park Service signage. Read the map at the Arrival Area before you commit to the long options.

The trees worth knowing by name

The Grizzly Giant is the one people come for. The Park Service calls it the largest sequoia in Yosemite and puts its age at roughly 2,900 years. Other published estimates run a little higher, which is the normal state of affairs for a tree nobody is going to cut down and count. Look at the first big limb rather than the trunk: it is thicker than the whole of most mature trees you have ever stood under, and it shows the scale better than the trunk does.

The California Tunnel Tree stands a short way past it, cut through in 1895 so that stagecoaches could be photographed driving through it. It is the only living tree in Yosemite you can still walk through. The more famous one, the Wawona Tunnel Tree, was cut in 1881 and fell in 1969 under a heavy snow load, and its stump is up in this same grove. The tunnel trees piece is the longer version of that story, which is less about vandalism than about what nineteenth-century Americans thought a national park was for.

The Bachelor and Three Graces are four trees growing so close that their root systems are effectively one, which is the practical argument for staying on the trail. Sequoia roots are shallow and wide, running out in a plate rather than down in a taproot, and the thing that kills a mature sequoia is almost never fire or disease. It is losing its footing.

Winter, when the road closes

Mariposa Grove Road closes to vehicles for the winter at the end of November and reopens when the snow allows, which has meant anywhere from early April onward in recent years. The reopening date moves with the snowpack and is not worth planning around more than a week out.

The grove itself does not close. The road and the trails stay open to foot travel, and walking, snowshoeing, or skiing the two miles up from the South Entrance makes a quiet February day. You will likely have the Grizzly Giant to yourself. Note that this is not a sanctioned snow-play area; the park's designated sledding hill is at Crane Flat, on the other side of the park. The winter guide has the rest of the cold-season picture.

What the 2018 restoration changed, and why

For three years the grove was closed entirely. It reopened on June 15, 2018. What came out: the lower-grove parking lot, the gift shop, the pit toilets, and the commercial tram tours that had run through the trees for decades. Roughly four acres of habitat were restored, boardwalks went in over the wet ground, and the wetland hydrology the roads had interrupted was reconnected. The bill came to about forty million dollars, split evenly between the Park Service and the Yosemite Conservancy.

The reason is a single finding from the park's own survey work. The overwhelming majority of young sequoias in this grove, juveniles and saplings both, were growing within about a hundred feet of wetlands. The seeds need bare, wet mineral soil to take. Paving the wet ground and routing traffic across it did not kill the big trees, which is why the damage went unnoticed for a century. It quietly removed the next generation. The piece on sequoias and fire is the natural history behind that, and the two failures are related: a grove can look healthy and produce no new trees.

The trams are not coming back. That is not a scheduling problem or a budget problem. It is the point of the project.

The rules that catch people out

Dogs are not allowed on any trail in the Mariposa Grove, including the paved ones. This is the rule that surprises people most, because Yosemite's general policy does allow leashed dogs on paved trails, and the Tuolumne Grove up on Highway 120 permits them on its paved section. The Mariposa Grove is an explicit exception. Dogs are fine in the parking lot and are not allowed on the shuttle, which means one adult stays with the car. The pets guide lays out where a dog can and cannot go in this park, and the answer is narrower than most owners expect.

Food stays within arm's reach. The picnic area at the plaza is a well-known bear spot. A daypack left on a table for three minutes is a daypack a bear has learned to check. The bear guide explains why a food-conditioned bear is usually a dead bear, which is the part that should change behavior.

Stay on the trail and off the root plates. See the Bachelor and Three Graces, above. Compacted soil over a sequoia's roots is a slow injury that nobody watches happen.

Bikes are allowed on Mariposa Grove Road but never on the trails, and only while the road is open to vehicles.

Buses and vehicles over about twenty-one feet need their own reservation. They book Mariposa Grove parking separately through recreation.gov; private cars do not. There is no separate fee for the grove beyond park entrance, and there is no park-wide vehicle reservation in effect for 2026.

Timing, and the parking lot

The Welcome Plaza lot holds a few hundred vehicles and fills on busy days. It is a smaller number than it sounds, because grove visits are long and the turnover is slow: a lot that holds three hundred cars and empties every three hours serves far fewer people than one that turns over every forty minutes. Various sites will tell you it fills by eight, or by nine, or that you must be there by seven thirty. None of those are Park Service figures. Early morning and late afternoon work. Midday on a summer weekend does not.

For the day itself, allow two hours as an absolute floor, and that only buys the shuttle both ways and the Big Trees Loop. Half a day is right for the Grizzly Giant Loop with time to stand around. A full day is the upper grove. Add the hour each way from the Valley if that is where you are staying, and consider whether the grove wants to be paired with Wawona rather than squeezed onto a Valley day. The one or two day guide takes a position on that trade.

The short version

  1. You cannot drive to the trees in season. Park at the Welcome Plaza by the South Entrance and take the free shuttle two miles up.
  2. The shuttle needs no reservation, runs about every fifteen minutes, and is wheelchair accessible. Its season runs roughly mid-April to the end of November, with no fixed dates published.
  3. A disability placard lets you drive the road. Accessible parking at the top is very limited.
  4. Big Trees Loop, three tenths of a mile and wheelchair accessible, is the everyone-can-do-it option. The Grizzly Giant Loop, about two miles, is the one to do if you do one.
  5. No dogs on any grove trail, paved included. This is an exception to the park's usual rule.
  6. In winter the road closes to cars and the grove stays open on foot. Snowshoe it.
  7. Allow two hours minimum, half a day to do it properly, plus an hour each way from the Valley.

The shuttle is part of the restoration. The old parking lot sat on the wet ground where young sequoias take root, and the two-mile ride costs visitors about fifteen minutes.
