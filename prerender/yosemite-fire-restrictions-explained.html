You bought a bundle of firewood at the gas station in Mariposa, because that is what you do before a Yosemite camping trip. You get to the site, unload the cooler, and find a ranger notice zip-tied to the fire ring: no wood fires until further notice. The bundle rides home unopened. Yosemite is under Stage 1 fire restrictions right now, and every summer a fresh set of visitors arrives not knowing that the rules changed since the last time they camped here, or that they ever change at all.

The restrictions are not complicated once someone lays them out, but nobody hands you the list at the entrance station. Here is what is banned, what still works, why the line sits where it does, and how to check whether any of this has changed by the time your trip starts.

What is in effect right now

Yosemite implemented Stage 1 fire restrictions at 12:01 a.m. on Friday, August 7, 2026, parkwide below 8,000 feet in elevation. The park cited hot, dry weather, low fuel moisture, and windy conditions raising the potential for fast wildfire growth, on the recommendation of the Yosemite Fire Program. The restrictions apply until further notice, which in practice means until conditions improve enough for the park to lift them, not on a fixed calendar date.

This is separate from, and layered on top of, the small lightning-caused fires that made news partway through August. An early-morning storm on August 12 started eight fires in the park, including ones near Aspen Valley, Dewey Point, and Wawona Road; the largest reported was a couple of acres. None triggered evacuations or site closures. They did not cause the ban. They came from the same dry conditions that triggered the restrictions a few days earlier, and they help explain why the park is in no hurry to lift it.

The rule has a cost when it is ignored. The Dome Fire was reported on the morning of September 15, about a quarter mile north of Chilnualna Falls above Wawona, while the restrictions were in force. On September 30 the local press reported that the Forest Service has found it human-caused, sparked by an abandoned campfire; the federal incident page still lists the cause as undetermined. The fire has closed Glacier Point Road and the Ostrander Lake Trail and everything south of it, and two helicopter pilots were killed fighting it on September 20. The Park Bulletin carries the closures as they stand, and Yosemite in October 2026 covers what the fire means for a trip this month.

What is actually banned

Below 8,000 feet, parkwide: no building, maintaining, attending, or using a wood or charcoal fire, including twig stoves, the small backpacking stoves that burn sticks instead of fuel. That covers a campfire at your site, a charcoal grill, a hibachi, anything that burns solid fuel.

Smoking is restricted too. Below 8,000 feet, smoking is prohibited except inside an enclosed vehicle, inside a campground or picnic area where wood and charcoal fires are otherwise permitted, or in a posted designated smoking area. That is a real change from ordinary conditions, and it catches people who assume the fire rules and the smoking rules are the same thing. They are related, but the smoking rule is broader.

What still works

You can still cook, and you can still have light and heat at your site. Portable stoves that burn pressurized gas, liquid fuel, or propane are permitted, and so are alcohol stoves, including the small tablet or cube stoves backpackers carry. If your plan for the trip was a camp stove for dinner and a headlamp for light, none of that changes.

Established fire rings in developed areas keep working, with one exception. Designated frontcountry campgrounds and picnic areas below 8,000 feet are exempt from the wood and charcoal ban, so a ring at Upper Pines or Wawona Campground can still hold a fire. Residential areas in the park's developed communities are exempt too, with one carve-out: in El Portal and Foresta, only charcoal fires are allowed, not wood, because those two communities carry the highest structure-fire exposure in the park. Everywhere else the exemption for residential and developed areas covers both.

Above 8,000 feet, in the wilderness, campfires are allowed in existing fire rings, up to 9,600 feet. That upper bound is a standing, year-round wilderness regulation rather than a fire-danger rule: above 9,600 feet, wood is scarce and slow to regenerate at that elevation, so fires are prohibited there regardless of fire restrictions. The 8,000-to-9,600-foot band is the one place in the park right now where a wood campfire, built in a ring that already exists, is legal. That is most of the high country above Tuolumne Meadows and along the Sierra crest, and almost none of the Valley, Wawona, or the areas most visitors camp in.

Two rules, not one, and they are not the same rule

Even outside fire restrictions, Yosemite Valley and Hodgdon Meadow run a standing seasonal rule: from May through September, wood fires there are allowed only in the evening, roughly 5 to 10 p.m. That rule exists for air quality, not fire danger. The Valley is a granite trough that traps cold air and smoke overnight, and a hundred and fifty morning campfires produce a haze the whole floor has to breathe. The campground guide covers that rule for a normal summer.

Stage 1 restrictions cancel that evening window. Right now, in the Valley and at Hodgdon Meadow, there is no evening exception, because the wood-fire ban is total below 8,000 feet regardless of the hour. When the restrictions lift, the standing evening-only rule comes back into force for whatever remains of the summer season.

Why the line sits at 8,000 feet, and why it moves

Fuel moisture, the amount of water held in dead grass, brush, and duff, drops fastest at lower, hotter, drier elevations. The Valley floor and the foothill approaches dry out first and stay driest longest. The high country holds moisture later into the season, sees fewer visitors overall, and has a shorter, cooler fire season by nature. Drawing the restriction line by elevation targets the most dangerous terrain instead of banning fires park-wide when only part of the park is at risk.

The number itself is not fixed. It reflects that year's conditions and gets set, and can be reset, by the park's fire managers as the season develops. In some past seasons the working elevation line has sat lower, closer to 6,000 feet, when conditions were more severe or a fire was actively burning nearby. In 2026 it is 8,000 feet. The mechanism is what stays constant: check the current line before you assume last year's trip rules still apply.

How to check before you drive up

Three places, and check them close to your trip rather than when you booked it, because restrictions can tighten, loosen, or shift elevation with a week's notice.

The park's own fire restrictions page (nps.gov/yose/planyourvisit/firerestrictions.htm) states whatever is currently in effect, in the same terms used here. The current conditions page (nps.gov/yose/planyourvisit/conditions.htm) rolls fire restrictions in with road and trail status, so it is worth a look regardless. And the Park Bulletin on this site tracks what the current NPS Yosemite Guide edition says is happening, updated roughly every five weeks, alongside the day-to-day alerts that move faster than that. None of these substitute for the others; the fire restrictions page is the one with the actual rule text.

What this means for your specific trip

Car camping in a Valley or Wawona campground. Bring a camp stove. Firewood bought at the gate is of no use right now. Marshmallows can be toasted over a canister stove, and a lot of campers do this anyway once they realize a stove is faster and does not leave you smelling like smoke in the tent.

Backpacking above 8,000 feet. You can build a fire in an existing ring, below 9,600 feet, if your route takes you that high. Most first and second nights out of trailheads at Tuolumne, Glacier Point Road, or the Valley rim start below that line, so plan on your stove for at least the early part of most routes. The backpacking guide covers permits, bear canisters, and the rest of what the paperwork does not tell you.

A day trip with a picnic. Grills in designated picnic areas are exempt, so a charcoal grill at a developed picnic site still works. A campfire ring you find along a trail or a beach that is not in a designated campground or picnic area does not count as an exemption, restrictions or not.

Smoking anywhere in the park below 8,000 feet. Car, permitted campground, or posted smoking area. Nowhere else, restrictions or not, and the fine-print addition during restrictions is that "permitted campground" now specifically means one where fires are still allowed under the current rules, which narrows the list.

This is a different question from whether your trip will be smoky

The two get confused often, but different pages answer them. Fire restrictions are about ignition: whether you personally can start a flame. Smoke is about air quality: whether a fire burning somewhere else, in the park or upwind of it, is putting particulate into the air you are breathing. You can have a trip under full fire restrictions with perfectly clear skies, and you can have a trip with no restrictions at all where the Valley is gray from a fire three counties away. Neither condition predicts the other.

The smoke season guide covers the second question in full: how to read an AQI number, where in the park the air tends to be cleanest on a bad day, and when to flex your itinerary uphill. This piece only answers the first one. If you are checking one page before a late-summer trip, check both; a clean-air day can still be a no-campfire day, and a smoky day does not excuse a fire that restrictions do not allow.

Violating the ban is not a technicality

Fire restriction violations are enforceable federal citations, and if an unattended or illegal fire escapes and requires a suppression response, the person responsible can be held liable for the cost of putting it out, which can far exceed any fine. Every fall, Yosemite's fire crews run planned burns specifically because prescribed fire keeps the forest from becoming the kind of fuel load that turns an escaped campfire into the summer's news story. A ranger who finds an unattended fire during Stage 1 restrictions is not treating it as a judgment call.

When restrictions usually lift

Restrictions like this one typically go into effect in July, after a stretch of hot, dry weather has pushed fuel moisture down, and they come off when temperatures drop and real rain falls, not on a schedule. Historically, Yosemite has close to a 90 percent chance of a season-ending precipitation event, defined as two or more inches of rain, by October 31. Recent examples: fire restrictions were rescinded in September 2022 after a cooling, wetter pattern moved in, and again in late October 2021 after a combination of rain, snow, and cooler temperatures reduced the danger enough for the park to lift them. Most years land somewhere in that window: sometime after Labor Day, before Halloween, tied to whichever storm actually delivers.

That overlaps almost exactly with the shoulder season this site already covers in detail. The fall guide walks through the sequence of closures that follows Labor Day, and the smoke season guide covers the separate, related question of air quality when a fire is actually burning nearby. Fire restrictions, smoke, and the shoulder-season closures are separate but move on roughly the same schedule, and a September or early-October trip can run into any combination of the three.

The practical version

Check the fire restrictions page in the days before you leave, not when you booked. Pack a camp stove regardless of what the restrictions say on the day you check, because conditions can tighten between booking and arrival. If a wood fire at 9 p.m. under the stars was the whole point of the trip for you, that experience is currently available only above 8,000 feet, in an existing ring, and it comes back everywhere else the week the park says the conditions allow it. Until then, cook on a stove.
