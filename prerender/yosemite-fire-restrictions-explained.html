You bought a bundle of firewood at the gas station in Mariposa, because that is what you do before a Yosemite camping trip. You get to the site, unload the cooler, and find a ranger notice zip-tied to the fire ring: no wood fires until further notice. The bundle rides home unopened. Yosemite was under Stage 1 fire restrictions from August 7, 2026 until this October, and it will be again the next time a summer turns hot and dry. Every summer a fresh set of visitors arrives not knowing the rules changed since they last camped here, or that rules ever change at all.

The restrictions are not complicated once someone lays them out, but nobody hands you the list at the entrance station. Here is what is banned, what still works, why the line sits where it does, and how to check whether any of this has changed by the time your trip starts.

What is in effect now: the normal rules only

No seasonal fire restrictions are in effect. The park's fire restrictions page, last updated October 22, 2026, lists none, and neither does its current conditions page: only the normal fire regulations that apply all year. The park has not published the day it lifted the 2026 order; it had been lifted by October 22.

That order took effect at 12:01 a.m. on Friday, August 7, 2026, parkwide below 8,000 feet in elevation. The park cited hot, dry weather, low fuel moisture, and windy conditions raising the potential for fast wildfire growth, on the recommendation of the Yosemite Fire Program. It applied until further notice, which in practice meant until conditions improved enough for the park to lift it, not a fixed calendar date.

The normal regulations, from the park's fire restrictions page, are the rules today. Fires are allowed only in grates, grills, existing fire rings, stoves and barbecue grills, must be attended, and must be put out completely. In Yosemite Valley campgrounds, picnic areas and Housekeeping Camp, and at Hodgdon Meadow Campground, fires are allowed only between 5 and 10 p.m. In the wilderness, campfires are allowed only in existing fire rings below 9,600 feet, and not within 100 feet of a trail or water, on top of Half Dome or El Capitan, in the Upper and Lower Cathedral Lakes basins, or within a quarter mile of the Kibbie Lake shoreline; at Little Yosemite Valley and the High Sierra Camp campgrounds, only in the rings the park provides. Burning giant sequoia material, leaves, needles, cones, or wet or green wood is prohibited, and fireworks always are.

This is separate from, and layered on top of, the small lightning-caused fires that made news partway through August. An early-morning storm on August 12 started eight fires in the park, including ones near Aspen Valley, Dewey Point, and Wawona Road; the largest reported was a couple of acres. None triggered evacuations or site closures. They did not cause the ban. They came from the same dry conditions that triggered the restrictions a few days earlier, and they help explain why the park kept the order in place into October.

The restrictions were in force when this fall's fire began. The Dome Fire was reported on the morning of September 15, about a quarter mile north of Chilnualna Falls above Wawona. What started it is not established: on September 30 the local press reported a Forest Service finding of an abandoned campfire, on October 6 the Forest Service withdrew that finding without giving a reason, and the federal incident page, InciWeb, lists the cause as undetermined. The fire closed Glacier Point Road in late September, until it reopened on October 10, and it has closed the Ostrander Lake Trail and everything south of it, and two helicopter pilots were killed fighting it on September 20. The Park Bulletin carries the closures as they stand, and Yosemite in October 2026 covers what the fire means for a trip this month.

What Stage 1 bans when it is in effect

Under the 2026 order, below 8,000 feet, parkwide: no building, maintaining, attending, or using a wood or charcoal fire, including twig stoves, the small backpacking stoves that burn sticks instead of fuel. That covers a campfire at your site, a charcoal grill, a hibachi, anything that burns solid fuel.

Smoking is restricted too. Under that order, anywhere in the park, smoking is prohibited except inside an enclosed vehicle, inside a campground or picnic area where wood and charcoal fires are otherwise permitted, in a posted designated smoking area, or while stopped in a barren or cleared spot at least three feet across. That is a real change from ordinary conditions, and it catches people who assume the fire rules and the smoking rules are the same thing. They are related, but the smoking rule is broader.

What still works

You can still cook, and you can still have light and heat at your site. Portable stoves that burn pressurized gas, liquid fuel, or propane are permitted, and so are alcohol stoves, including the small tablet or cube stoves backpackers carry. If your plan for the trip was a camp stove for dinner and a headlamp for light, none of that changes.

Established fire rings in developed areas keep working, with one exception. Designated frontcountry campgrounds and picnic areas below 8,000 feet are exempt from the wood and charcoal ban, so a ring at Upper Pines or Wawona Campground can still hold a fire. Residential areas in the park's developed communities are exempt too, with one carve-out: in El Portal and Foresta, only charcoal fires are allowed, not wood. Everywhere else the exemption for residential and developed areas covers both.

Above 8,000 feet, in the wilderness, campfires are allowed in existing fire rings, up to 9,600 feet. That upper bound is a standing, year-round wilderness regulation rather than a fire-danger rule: above 9,600 feet, wood is scarce and slow to regenerate at that elevation, so fires are prohibited there regardless of fire restrictions. Under the 2026 order, the 8,000-to-9,600-foot band was the one place in the wilderness where a wood campfire, built in a ring that already exists, was legal. That is most of the high country above Tuolumne Meadows and along the Sierra crest, and almost none of the Valley, Wawona, or the areas most visitors camp in.

Two rules, not one, and they are not the same rule

Even outside fire restrictions, the park runs a standing hours rule: fires are allowed only between 5 and 10 p.m. at Yosemite Valley campgrounds, picnic areas and Housekeeping Camp, and at Hodgdon Meadow Campground. It is a rule about when, not about fire danger. The campground guide covers that rule for a normal summer.

The two stack. Designated frontcountry campgrounds are exempt from the Stage 1 wood and charcoal ban, so a Valley or Hodgdon Meadow ring could still hold a fire under the 2026 order, but only inside the 5 to 10 p.m. window. A Valley picnic area follows the same hours if its fire grates are open to wood or charcoal.

Why the line sits at 8,000 feet, and why it moves

Fuel moisture, the amount of water held in dead grass, brush, and duff, drops fastest at lower, hotter, drier elevations. The Valley floor and the foothill approaches dry out first and stay driest longest. The high country holds moisture later into the season, sees fewer visitors overall, and has a shorter, cooler fire season by nature. Drawing the restriction line by elevation targets the most dangerous terrain instead of banning fires park-wide when only part of the park is at risk.

The number itself is not fixed. It reflects that year's conditions and gets set, and can be reset, by the park's fire managers as the season develops. In some past seasons the working elevation line has sat lower, closer to 6,000 feet, when conditions were more severe or a fire was actively burning nearby. In 2026 it was 8,000 feet. The mechanism is what stays constant: check the current line before you assume last year's trip rules still apply.

How to check before you drive up

Three places, and check them close to your trip rather than when you booked it, because restrictions can tighten, loosen, or shift elevation with a week's notice.

The park's own fire restrictions page (nps.gov/yose/planyourvisit/firerestrictions.htm) states whatever is currently in effect, in the same terms used here. The current conditions page (nps.gov/yose/planyourvisit/conditions.htm) rolls fire restrictions in with road and trail status, so it is worth a look regardless. And the Park Bulletin on this site tracks what the current NPS Yosemite Guide edition says is happening, updated roughly every five weeks, alongside the day-to-day alerts that move faster than that. None of these substitute for the others; the fire restrictions page is the one with the actual rule text.

What this means for your specific trip

Car camping in a Valley or Wawona campground. With no restrictions in force, the campground fire ring is usable, inside the 5 to 10 p.m. window in the Valley and at Hodgdon Meadow. Bring a camp stove anyway: it is faster, it does not leave you smelling like smoke in the tent, and restrictions can return with little notice.

Backpacking. With no restrictions in force, a wood fire is legal in an existing ring anywhere below 9,600 feet, outside the places the standing rules exclude. Under Stage 1 the floor rises to 8,000 feet, and most first and second nights out of trailheads at Hetch Hetchy, the Valley or Wawona start below that line, so the stove carries the early part of most routes. The backpacking guide covers permits, bear canisters, and the rest of what the paperwork does not tell you.

A day trip with a picnic. Grills in designated picnic areas are exempt even under Stage 1, so a charcoal grill at a developed picnic site still works. A campfire ring you find along a trail or a beach that is not in a designated campground or picnic area does not count as an exemption, restrictions or not.

Smoking anywhere in the park. Under the normal rules: not in public buildings, not within 25 feet of a building other than a single-family residence, and not while moving on a trail (stop, and stay put until it is out). Under Stage 1: car, permitted campground, posted smoking area, or a stopped-and-cleared spot at least three feet across. Nowhere else. During restrictions, "permitted campground" specifically means one where fires are still allowed under the current rules, which narrows the list.

This is a different question from whether your trip will be smoky

The two get confused often, but different pages answer them. Fire restrictions are about ignition: whether you personally can start a flame. Smoke is about air quality: whether a fire burning somewhere else, in the park or upwind of it, is putting particulate into the air you are breathing. You can have a trip under full fire restrictions with perfectly clear skies, and you can have a trip with no restrictions at all where the Valley is gray from a fire three counties away. Neither condition predicts the other.

The smoke season guide covers the second question in full: how to read an AQI number, where in the park the air tends to be cleanest on a bad day, and when to flex your itinerary uphill. This piece only answers the first one. If you are checking one page before a late-summer trip, check both; a clean-air day can still be a no-campfire day, and a smoky day does not excuse a fire that restrictions do not allow.

Violating the ban is not a technicality

Fire restriction violations are enforceable federal citations, and if an unattended or illegal fire escapes and requires a suppression response, the person responsible can be held liable for the cost of putting it out, which can far exceed any fine. Every fall, Yosemite's fire crews run planned burns specifically because prescribed fire keeps the forest from becoming the kind of fuel load that turns an escaped campfire into the summer's news story. A ranger who finds an unattended fire during Stage 1 restrictions is not treating it as a judgment call.

When restrictions usually lift

Restrictions like this one typically go into effect in July, after a stretch of hot, dry weather has pushed fuel moisture down, and they come off when temperatures drop and real rain falls, not on a schedule. Recent examples: fire restrictions were rescinded on September 21, 2022 after a cooling, wetter pattern moved in, and on October 20, 2021 after a combination of rain, snow, and cooler temperatures reduced the danger enough for the park to lift them. Most years land somewhere in that window: sometime after Labor Day, before Halloween, tied to whichever storm actually delivers. In 2026 the order that took effect on August 7 had been lifted by October 22.

That overlaps almost exactly with the shoulder season this site already covers in detail. The fall guide walks through the sequence of closures that follows Labor Day, and the smoke season guide covers the separate, related question of air quality when a fire is actually burning nearby. Fire restrictions, smoke, and the shoulder-season closures are separate but move on roughly the same schedule, and a September or early-October trip can run into any combination of the three.

The practical version

Check the fire restrictions page in the days before you leave, not when you booked. Pack a camp stove regardless of what the restrictions say on the day you check, because conditions can tighten between booking and arrival. If a wood fire at 9 p.m. under the stars was the whole point of the trip for you, the 2026 restrictions are lifted, so it is back in the campground rings and in the wilderness below 9,600 feet, under the standing rules. The next time Stage 1 is set, it retreats to existing rings between 8,000 and 9,600 feet, and below that line the stove does the cooking until the park lifts the order.
