Most people who look up the weather in Yosemite look for one number, and the park does not have one. Yosemite runs from about 2,000 feet to about 13,000, and most of what visitors do happens at two very different heights: the Valley floor, at about 4,000 feet, and the high country around Tuolumne Meadows, at 8,600. On a July afternoon the Valley averages 89°F while Tuolumne Meadows, about an hour and a half's drive up Tioga Road, averages 72°F, and that night the meadow drops toward 39°F while the Valley stays near 57°F. A packing list built on one of those numbers is wrong for the other. This is the year in both places, from the Park Service's own long-term averages, with what each month's weather does to the roads, the waterfalls, the trails and the air.

One caution before the numbers. They are normals: thirty-year averages, 1991 to 2020, that describe a typical month and promise nothing about a given week. A March can bring a foot of snow to the Valley floor or a run of 70-degree afternoons. Use the averages to decide when to go and what to bring, and the forecast to decide what to do on the day.

Two weather stations, 4,600 feet apart

The Park Service publishes monthly averages for two places: Yosemite Valley, at 4,000 feet, and Tuolumne Meadows, at 8,600 feet. The park's season pages group Wawona with the Valley at the same 4,000 feet, so the Valley column stands for the south end of the park as well. Hetch Hetchy, at 3,900 feet, sits a hundred feet lower still, and the Park Service credits it with one of the longest hiking seasons in the park. Glacier Point and the rest of the Glacier Point Road fall between the two columns, and Tioga Pass, at 9,945 feet, sits above both.

Three things in that table do most of the planning work.

First, the gap between the two columns is not constant. From April through September the meadow's average high runs 16 to 19 degrees below the Valley's, the widest in August, when the Valley averages 89°F and Tuolumne 70°F. In winter the gap closes: 7 degrees in January, 6 in December. That is the summer argument for driving up Tioga Road on a hot day, and it is also why the high country is no refuge from the cold in winter, when the road to it is closed anyway.

Second, the nights are what catch people. Tuolumne's average low is at or below freezing in nine months of the year; only June, July and August average above 32°F, and June only by a degree. In the Valley a summer day swings about 30 degrees from dawn to afternoon, 57°F to 89°F in July and 51°F to 83°F in September, so the visitor who leaves the lodge in shorts at 6 am to beat the crowds is cold for the first hour. In December and January the Valley's swing narrows to under 20 degrees, and its average low sits just below freezing in December, January and February.

Third, almost all the water falls in half the year. The Valley's monthly averages add up to about 37 inches of precipitation, and the Park Service says about 95 percent of the park's precipitation falls between October and May, more than 75 percent of it between November and March. January is the wettest month on the Valley floor at 7.0 inches; August is the driest at 0.2. The Valley's normals are in fact wetter than Tuolumne's, which add up to about 30 inches.

The elevation ladder

Every trip to Yosemite climbs or descends a version of the same ladder, and the rung matters more than the calendar. From the bottom:

  • Hetch Hetchy, 3,900 feet. The low corner, with the reservoir and its waterfalls. Its trails are among the first in the park to lose their snow, often by April, and the park's advice for them is plenty of water, sunscreen and an eye out for rattlesnakes.
  • Yosemite Valley and Wawona, 4,000 feet. The Valley column of the table. Both are open by car all year, and a winter storm here can arrive as rain or as snow.
  • Glacier Point, about 7,200 feet. The Park Service puts the overlook 3,214 feet above Curry Village on the Valley floor. No monthly normals are published for it, but it sits much closer to Tuolumne's elevation than the Valley's, and the road to it is closed by snow from about November until it is plowed open in spring.
  • Tuolumne Meadows, 8,600 feet. The high-country column of the table, reached in summer and fall on Tioga Road.
  • Tioga Pass, 9,945 feet. The park's east entrance, on the crest of the Sierra Nevada, at the top of Tioga Road.
  • The summits, to about 13,000 feet. The park's top elevation, reached only on foot, where the table's Tuolumne column is the optimistic case.

The practical rule follows: a day that starts on the Valley floor and ends at Glacier Point or Tuolumne Meadows crosses two climates, and the bag should be packed for the higher one.

The year, month by month

The months group into six stretches that behave alike. The figures are the table's; the consequences come from the Park Service's season, road and waterfall pages.

December, January, February: the cold, wet core

The Valley averages a high of 46°F in December, 48°F in January and 51°F in February, with lows of 28°F to 30°F, so December, not January, is the coldest month by day. These are also the three wettest months, between 5.6 and 7.0 inches each. Tuolumne averages highs of 39°F to 41°F and lows of 12°F to 13°F, but nobody drives there: Tioga Road closes for the season, usually sometime in November, and no vehicles are allowed between Crane Flat and Tioga Pass until it reopens. Glacier Point Road closes in November too, though from mid-December through early April the park plows it as far as the Badger Pass Ski Area. Most of the park lies under snow from about November through May. The Valley floor may or may not have snow on any given day, Yosemite Falls begins flowing again once some rain and snow have fallen, and in winter an ice cone builds at the base of the upper fall. Late December and early January are busy, with the holiday traffic and limited parking the park warns about.

March and April: the thaw, unevenly

The Valley climbs from an average high of 57°F in March to 63°F in April, with lows of 34°F and 38°F, and the precipitation falls off from 5.5 inches to 3.2. Tuolumne is still deep winter at 42°F and 45°F by day. The Park Service calls spring weather highly variable, with warm days and the occasional winter storm, and says tire chains are sometimes required even in spring. Some hikes from the Valley, Wawona and Hetch Hetchy are often snow-free by April; most of the park is not. The waterfalls are building, and the park says roaring runoff at Yosemite Falls runs April through June.

May and June: high water, high roads

May averages 71°F and 45°F in the Valley, June 81°F and 51°F, and the precipitation drops to 1.9 inches and then half an inch. This is the waterfall season: peak runoff typically comes in May or June, and Yosemite Falls usually peaks in May. Tuolumne warms to 54°F and then 65°F by day, though its June low of 33°F is barely above freezing. The high roads open in this window when the plows finish: the Park Service says Glacier Point Road typically opens sometime in May and Tioga Road in late May or June, and plowing on both typically begins around April 15. Most Valley, Wawona and Hetch Hetchy trails are snow-free by sometime in May, and the higher trails can stay snowy into June or July.

July and August: hot below, mild above

The Valley averages 89°F and 57°F in both months, with a third of an inch of rain or less. Tuolumne averages 72°F in July and 70°F in August, the only stretch of the year when its nights average well above freezing. The waterfalls are falling: some, Yosemite Falls among them, are often a trickle or dry by August, while Vernal, Nevada and Bridalveil flow all year at a much lower volume. Rain, when it comes, is usually an afternoon thundershower at the higher elevations, and it brings lightning, covered below. The high country's wildflower season begins around July. Our guide to Tuolumne Meadows in a day is built for this stretch.

September and October: the long dry fall

September is still summer in the Valley, 83°F and 51°F, with under half an inch of rain. October drops to 71°F and 41°F, and its 1.6 inches are the most precipitation of any month since May. Tuolumne falls to 65°F and then 57°F by day, with lows of 32°F and 26°F. The Park Service calls fall weather quite variable, from hot to cold and dry to rainy or snowy. All areas of the park usually stay open through October, but short-term closures for snow can happen, services along Tioga Road are not available after September, and overnight parking along it is not permitted after October 14. The waterfalls run low, many with little or no water. The deciduous trees, the big-leaf maples, black oaks and dogwoods, tend to be at their showiest around mid-October.

November: the door closes

November averages 56°F and 33°F in the Valley, a fifteen-degree drop in the high from October, the sharpest month-to-month fall of the year, and the precipitation jumps to 4.1 inches. Tioga and Glacier Point Roads usually close sometime this month, and the Valley and Wawona settle into their winter shape: open by car, with chains possible on any day a storm comes through.

Afternoon thunderstorms

The summer rain the table barely registers is the most dangerous weather in the park. The Park Service says thunderstorms are most common from June through September and can happen any time of year, and that from July through September the North American monsoon can pull moisture from the Gulf of California into the Sierra Nevada. They are most common in the afternoon, when the sun heats the ground and towering cumulus clouds build over the ridges. In July 1985 lightning at Half Dome killed two people and injured three.

The park's guidance is specific. Watch for the afternoon build-up of cumulus clouds; if you hear thunder, the storm is within about 10 miles, so go. Above the treeline or along the rim, hike in the morning and reach summits before noon. Avoid viewpoints, isolated trees, water and metal, and stay inside a substantial building or a hard-topped vehicle until 30 minutes after the last thunder. The only safe place, the park says, is inside one or the other.

Altitude, for a night at 8,600 feet

The Valley is too low to matter for altitude. Tuolumne Meadows is not. The CDC's guidance for travelers puts any unacclimatized person who sleeps at about 8,000 feet or higher at risk of acute mountain sickness, whose cardinal symptom is a headache, usually with fatigue, dizziness, loss of appetite or nausea, and notes that it affects about a quarter of visitors who sleep above 8,000 feet in Colorado. Symptoms usually start 2 to 12 hours after arriving, and they generally resolve within 12 to 48 hours if the traveler does not climb higher. Descending 1,000 feet or more usually brings rapid relief.

For a Yosemite trip, that means three things. The CDC frames the risk by where you sleep, so a day trip from the Valley to Tuolumne and back carries less of it than a night up high. A night at Tuolumne Meadows or above is the exposure that matters, and the first one is the night to take easy. And the Tuolumne trailheads sit at about 8,600 feet, so a hiker who drove up from sea level that morning is starting a hike at altitude, unacclimatized.

Smoke, the season the table leaves out

The normals say nothing about air, and in late summer the air can matter more than the temperature. The Park Service says hazy or smoky conditions are common in summer from fires in the park or elsewhere in California, with one to three weeks of unhealthy or worse air quality common in August and September. Lightning starts fires naturally in the Sierra Nevada, the park runs prescribed burns when conditions allow, and smoke can turn up in spring, fall and even winter. A trip in late summer should carry a plan for a smoky day; our guide to Yosemite in smoke season covers the air-quality maps and when it pays to drive higher.

The weather you drive through

The table's cold months become a driving question. The Park Service says some park roads are closed from around November through May or June, and that tire chains may be required on open roads from around October through April, depending on conditions. When chains are required, you must carry them and know how to use them, regardless of the type of vehicle you drive. Our tire chain guide explains the three requirement levels, rental cars and electric vehicles. The Valley and Wawona stay reachable by car all year; Tioga Road and Glacier Point Road close, which is also why the Tuolumne column of the table is, for most of the winter, a description of a place you cannot drive to.

Using the numbers

Pack for the spread, not the average. A July day that starts at dawn in the Valley and ends at Glacier Point or Tuolumne covers the 57°F Valley morning, the 89°F Valley afternoon and, at Tuolumne Meadows, an afternoon that averages 17 degrees cooler and a night near 39°F. A fleece, a rain shell and a warm hat cover the whole of it, and in summer the shell is for the afternoon storm, not the forecast.

Choose the month by what you came for. The waterfalls peak in May and June; the high country is open and warm in July, August and September; the color is best around mid-October; the snow and the empty Valley belong to December through February. Our guide to when to visit Yosemite weighs those against the crowds, and the waterfalls guide takes the falls one by one.

Then check the day. The averages are the climate; what decides the morning is the forecast, the road status and the air.
