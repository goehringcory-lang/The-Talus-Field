The Park Service hosts a series of cultural demonstrations at the Yosemite Museum, run in 2026 as Connecting to Traditions from July into October, and it is one of the few places in the park to learn from Indigenous practitioners directly. Indigenous artists demonstrate basket weaving, beadwork, acorn preparation, and stone tool knapping, the living practices of the peoples who have tended this valley for thousands of years, long before it was a park or was named Yosemite. The demonstrations are free and open to all. The 2026 series ended with its October 14 to 15 session, and the 2027 dates had not been published as of October 21, 2026.

The 2026 schedule

Demonstrations take place at or just outside the Yosemite Museum in Yosemite Village, or in the Indian Village behind it, next to the visitor center, shuttle stop 5. Times were posted at the Museum rather than fixed in advance. Everything here was free.

  • July 17 to 18, Basket Weaving and Acorn. Chrissy Atwell, Big Sandy Rancheria.
  • August 7 to 9, Basketry and Beading. Charlene Redner and Rena Brown, Bishop Paiute.
  • August 15 to 16, Knap-in Event. Skilled tool makers demonstrating the art of knapping.
  • August 15 to 17, Basket Weaving. Sandy Clark, North Fork Rancheria of Mono Indians.
  • September 9 to 10, Basket Weaving. Mary Cruz, North Fork Rancheria of Mono Indians.
  • October 14 to 15, Basket Weaving. Christina McDonald, North Fork Rancheria of Mono Indians.

These were the 2026 dates. For next season's schedule, ask at the Museum or check the Park Service's Yosemite site.

Why this is worth your afternoon

Yosemite has seven traditionally associated tribes, and the land you drive through was shaped by them: the black oaks kept open for acorns, the meadows burned to keep them meadows, the plant materials gathered and worked into baskets so fine they hold water. Most visitors move through all of that without ever meeting the people who carry it. Connecting to Traditions closes that gap. You get to stand a few feet from a weaver splitting sedge root or a knapper striking a blade from obsidian, and you get to ask questions of the tribal members doing the work.

That is a rare thing, and a generous one on the part of the artists. It is easy to treat a national park as scenery. These demonstrations are the reminder that it is also a homeland, tended by living cultures who are still here, still practicing, and willing to share an hour with whoever shows up. When the series returns, put the Museum on your list. It is among the best free hours in the park, and it does something a viewpoint cannot.

The demonstrations sit alongside the park's free ranger programs as the interpretive heart of a Yosemite visit. Take both if you can.
