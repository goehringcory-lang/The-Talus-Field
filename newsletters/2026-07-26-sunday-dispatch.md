# The Sunday Dispatch

**Sunday, July 26, 2026 · From the Nature Notes archive**

Every issue of *Yosemite Nature Notes* from 1922 to 2003 is now readable on this
site: 512 issues, about 1.87 million words, written mostly by rangers and
seasonal naturalists who were standing in the park when they wrote it. This
week we pulled the late-July issues. Six of them have something useful to say
about the park you would walk into today.

---

## 1. The Merced is a garden right now, and it closes in a week

Richard J. Hartesveldt, a ranger naturalist, spent part of the summer of 1953
wading the Merced in a bathing suit and tennis shoes, "for the sake of one's
feet," cataloging the flowering plants that live in moving water. Nobody looks
at them. Fishermen don't, he noted, because in a mountain stream they are
barely cover for trout.

The one he liked best was the floating-leaf pondweed, or floating brownleaf,
whose broad ovate leaves hinge to long slender stems and whose flowers push
just far enough above the surface to be pollinated by wind. He gives the
address: the south side of the river, a few hundred yards upstream from
Sentinel Bridge. Early in the summer the current carries the leaves as far
downstream as their stems will allow.

Then the line that makes this a late-July item rather than a June one: "Most of
this colony is high and dry by the first week in August."

You have about a week. Wade in with something on your feet.

*Source: [Yosemite Nature Notes, July 1953 (Vol. 32, No. 7)](/archive/1953/vol-32-no-7/), "Flowers of the Merced's Flowing Water."*

## 2. Seven trees on one rock

Ranger-naturalist Ernest A. Payne wrote up Lembert Dome in July 1938 as a
correction to the obvious reading of it. The dome, he says, "thrusts its bald
pate above the verdure of Tuolumne Meadows," and a visitor is meant to dismiss
it as barren. Payne counted seven conifer species on it and allowed that there
may be more.

Lodgepole pine, which owns the meadow. Mountain hemlock in numbers near the
talus on the northwest side, every size from first-year seedlings to old
patriarchs. Western white pine on the northwest shoulder, which in the summer
of 1937 hung in clusters of golden-rust cones. Whitebark pine, scattered near
the summit, distinguishable from the lodgepole by counting needles per bundle:
lodgepole two, whitebark five. Sierra juniper, "misshapen, distorted," which he
rates one of the best things on the Sierran skyline. Red fir on the east slope.

And a Jeffrey pine near the top of the south shoulder, not three feet tall,
"struggling with its feet deeply embedded in a crack in the solid granite,"
gnarled enough that Payne read real age into it. He compares it to the dwarf
conifers gardeners train for temple courtyards, one produced by nature and one
by control.

All seven are cone-bearing. No broadleaf tree grows on the dome at all.

The summit is about 800 feet above the meadow floor. Check road status on
[The Park Bulletin](/now) before you plan around it.

*Source: [Yosemite Nature Notes, July 1938 (Vol. 17, No. 7)](/archive/1938/vol-17-no-7/), "Trees on Lembert Dome."*

## 3. A test for the night sky, from 1948

F. Robert Kirschman, a museum assistant, opened his 1948 piece with a
complaint that still lands: visitors are impressed by the daylight and miss the
nights.

His argument was technical. Atmosphere, not glass, is the limit. Griffith
Observatory in Los Angeles had a fine 12-inch Zeiss refractor and had to run it
at 125 diameters because the air over the city would not support more. In
Yosemite, he wrote, you could push a good 3-inch portable past that, and a
3-inch was within reach of an ordinary amateur.

Then the part you can use without owning anything. Kirschman reports splitting
Epsilon Lyrae into a double with his unaided eye, both at Tenaya Lake and in
Yosemite Valley, "a feat usually considered possible only with optical aid."
It sits right next to Vega, which is high overhead on a late-July night. If you
can separate it, the air above you is genuinely good.

He spent that night on the northeast shore of Tenaya. Venus and Jupiter came
out "like two great jewels," doubled in the water. Before dawn, Orion.

*Source: [Yosemite Nature Notes, July 1948 (Vol. 27, No. 7)](/archive/1948/vol-27-no-7/), "Stars Over Yosemite."*

## 4. The great gray owl and the man gathering bark for the Firefall

Ranger-naturalist Cope Jensen drove Valley to Glacier Point on a July day in
1931 and wrote down everything that moved. Deer all along the road. Five
four-point bucks at Peregoy Meadows. A weasel crossing with a chipmunk held
head-up, moving in a gallop.

A few miles past Bridalveil Creek came the find. A great gray owl, flushed by
Don Butterbaugh while he was gathering red fir bark for the Firefall at Glacier
Point. It went behind a clump of firs and that was all they got of it. The
species had not been reported there in four years, and Jensen notes it was near
that same ground, per Grinnell and Storer's *Animal Life in the Yosemite*, that
the great gray owl was first found nesting south of Canada in 1915, at 7,400
feet, within a mile south of Ostrander Rocks.

Read that sentence again for what it takes for granted. The Firefall ran on red
fir bark, and somebody's job was to go get it.

The rest of the drive dates just as hard. Two mother "hold-up" bears with two
cubs each between Ostrander Rocks and Washburn Turn, "exceedingly tame and
quite aggressive in their quest for candy and cookies," and thin for it, Jensen
thought, or because of it. A gray fox with a bad hind foot at the garbage pits
a mile from Glacier Point.

The owl is still there. The Firefall, the hand-fed bears, and the garbage pits
are not.

*Source: [Yosemite Nature Notes, August 1931 (Vol. 10, No. 8)](/archive/1931/vol-10-no-8/), "Motoring to Glacier Point with a Naturalist."*

## 5. Trailitis, 1935

Helen K. Sharsmith and Jane Morton McIntire diagnosed themselves in July 1935
with "an acute attack of trailitis," brought on by "a prolonged, incautious,
and injurious perusal of the topo map." Treatment was four days into the Ten
Lakes Basin with food and sleeping bags on pack frames and no stock, which they
defend on the grounds that burros force you to fix an itinerary in advance.

They went up the Yosemite Falls zig-zags, a day and a half up Yosemite Creek
canyon, over the divide into the Grand Canyon of the Tuolumne, then Ten Lakes
to the South Fork of Cathedral Creek, around Tuolumne Peak at 10,000 feet, and
down to May Lake. That last stretch was a new trail, unfinished and unsigned
when they walked it, and easy to follow anyway.

The detail worth keeping: on the climb out of Cathedral Creek they found
raindrop craters in the trail dust, and the only prints in it were a bear's. No
human tracks at all. At the high pass, in the shadow of Tuolumne Peak, a pika
came out and worked a few feet from them.

They report the cure only partly took. "The chronic ailment still persists in
insidious form."

*Source: [Yosemite Nature Notes, July 1935 (Vol. 14, No. 7)](/archive/1935/vol-14-no-7/), "Trailitis: A Cure."*

## 6. One hundred years ago this Friday

The issue dated July 31, 1926 turns a century old on Friday. Two things in it.

An unsigned note, initialed H. C. B., reports a deer count on the Glacier Point
road as far as Bridalveil Creek on the Fourth of July. Morning run, 5:30 to
8:00: twenty-seven deer, including a doe with two fawns two or three days old,
and almost no bucks. Evening run, 6:00 to 8:00, same road: fifty-four, with the
antlered bucks now showing. The does had moved to the meadow edges and the big
bucks were in the chinquapin brush on the ridges.

Two counts, one road, one day, and a two-to-one difference. The finding is
about when you drive, not how many deer there are.

The issue opens with Joseph Grinnell's creed on wildlife administration,
reprinted from *Science*. Point three: "I believe it is unwise to attempt the
absolute extermination of any native vertebrate species whatsoever." That was a
position that needed arguing in 1926.

*Source: [Yosemite Nature Notes, July 31, 1926 (Vol. 5, No. 7)](/archive/1926/vol-5-no-7/).*

## Three things to do with this week

1. **Get in the Merced upstream of Sentinel Bridge** before the first week of
   August, and look down instead of up.
2. **Find Vega after full dark and try Epsilon Lyrae** with your eyes only. It
   is a fair test of the air.
3. **Drive a road twice in one day**, early and late, and count what you see.
   H. C. B. got a two-to-one spread on one stretch of road in a single day.
